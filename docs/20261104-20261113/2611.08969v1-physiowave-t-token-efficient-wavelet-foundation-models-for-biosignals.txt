# PhysioWave-T: Token-Efficient Wavelet Foundation Models for Biosignals 

Yanlong Chen, Simone Benatti, Luca Benini, Fellow, IEEE, Cuntai Guan, Fellow, IEEE, and Yawei Li 

Abstract —We present PhysioWave-T, a token-efficient wavelet foundation-model family for electroencephalography (EEG), electrocardiography (ECG), and surface electromyography (sEMG). Dynamic scale folding recombines the J+1 wavelet subbands before tokenization, compressing the sequence from (J+1) CS to CS tokens for J decomposition levels, C channels, and S temporal patches. Route-specific wavelet front ends and learned channel-name embeddings let one Transformer per modality accommodate diverse channel counts and sampling rates, while unseen configurations reuse the pre-trained backbone via a lightweight front end. Together, these components scale PhysioWave to EEG: we pre-train a 30M-parameter EEG foundation model on over 15M windows spanning 19 to 128 channels, alongside ECG (30M) and sEMG (12M) encoders. Across four EEG routes, folding cuts Transformer latency by 4.0–7.2 × and peak memory by 6.8–12.7 × over scale expansion without accuracy loss. The EEG encoder leads eight of nine metrics against BENDR, BIOT, LaBraM, and EEGPT, and surpasses LUNA-L on wearable seizure detection with fewer parameters. Reliability-aware latent fusion (RALF) then integrates summary tokens from the independently pre-trained encoders using per-sample reliability weights. On SeizeIT2, fusion raises event sensitivity at 11 false alarms/h from 61.4 % to 74.4 % with RALF, while RALF boosts window-level AUPRC from 0.321 to 0.399 over the best unimodal model and outperforms weighted-logit and concatenation fusion in AUPRC across all six training variants. PhysioWave-T also sets new state-of-the-art sEMG accuracies and the highest macro-F1 on PTB-XL Super and Chapman–Shaoxing, establishing an efficient, extensible foundation for biosignal representation learning. Code and models are available at https://github.com/ForeverBlue816/PhysioWave. 

Index Terms —Biosignal foundation models, self-supervised learning, token efficiency, heterogeneous acquisition, multimodal fusion.

1 INTRODUCTION 

# PHYSIOLOGICAL signals reflect the underlying dynamics of the human body across diverse temporal scales, yet their acquisition hardware and recording protocols are far from uniform. Across clinical and consumer settings, EEG spans two-channel wearables to 128-channel scalp arrays; ECG ranges from single-lead ambulatory patches to clinical twelve-lead systems; and sEMG is captured by wristbands, forearm arrays, and high-density grids, each governed by a distinct sampling rate and sensor geometry. A truly reusable representation must capture multiscale physiological structure while flexibly accommodating this hardware diversity, yet these two desiderata pull directly against each other. Wavelet decompositions are well suited to non-stationary biosignals, but exposing every scale to a Transformer incurs a steep quadratic cost: a J-level decomposition of C channels and S patches yields (J+1) CS

Mapping every recording onto a single fixed input layout sidesteps this variability, but intermediate heuristics such as channel selection, padding, or resampling inevitably discard acquisition-specific information along the way. Our prior works, WaveFormer [1] and PhysioWave [2], learn multiscale representations of ECG and sEMG through adaptive wavelet selection, soft gating, cross-scale feature aggregation, and frequency-guided masked pre-training. Nevertheless, three design choices in PhysioWave limit how far this paradigm scales. Its tokenizer expanded every wavelet scale into a separate token row, and each encoder was constrained to a single fixed input configuration. Both limitations are most severe for EEG, whose clinical montages vary widely across datasets and whose dense electrode arrays rapidly multiply the token count. PhysioWave’s multimodal experiments relied on an external pre-trained backbone and combined modalities using fixed learned weights that could not adapt to the signal quality of individual samples. These bottlenecks raise three central questions that anchor this work: how to retain multiscale time-frequency information without multiplying tokens, how to share a single architec-ture across heterogeneous acquisition setups, and how to fuse modalities whose reliability varies across recordings. PhysioWave-T addresses the first two questions by de-coupling acquisition-specific processing from representation learning. Each acquisition route, defined by a channel count and sampling rate, receives a dedicated wavelet front end and sampling-rate-specific patch projection, while sharing a single Transformer backbone and a name-based channel vocabulary across the modality. Within each front end, dynamic scale folding recombines wavelet subbands prior to tokenization using weights predicted from local arXiv:2611.08969v1 [cs.AI] 6 Nov 2026 IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON PATTERN ANALYSIS AND MACHINE INTELLIGENCE 2

(J+1) CS tokens regardless of decomposition depth: deeper analysis enriches individual tokens instead of multiplying sequence length. Because folding contains no channel-specific parameters, it transfers unchanged across montages; an unseen downstream configuration requires only attaching a lightweight front end to the frozen or fine-tuned backbone and appending any new sensor names to the vocabulary. These mechanisms allow PhysioWave to scale to EEG, yielding a unified EEG foundation model as a central contribution of this work. Without coercing recordings into a common montage, we pre-train a 30M-parameter encoder on over 15 M four-second windows spanning clinical, research, brain–computer interface, and high-density corpora (19–128 channels at 256 and 512 Hz). Across four EEG routes, scale folding reduces Transformer latency by 4.0–7.2 × and peak memory by 6.8–12.7 × over scale expansion, cutting per-batch memory on 128-channel recordings from ∼32 GB to 2.5 GB. Against BENDR, BIOT, LaBraM, and EEGPT, our encoder leads on eight of nine metrics across sleep staging, P300, and error-related negativity detection—including on Sleep-EDFx, whose 100 Hz derivations are unseen during pre-training. On two-channel behind-the-ear seizure detec-tion, it reaches 61.4 % sensitivity at ≤11 false alarms per hour, outperforming LUNA-L with 25.4 M versus 40.4 M parameters. Pre-training also boosts label efficiency: using just 10 % of KaggleERN labels, the encoder surpasses a fully supervised scratch model. Finally, the learned representations capture intrinsic physiology: channel embeddings recover scalp topography without coordinate supervision, and the slowest wavelet scale’s weight progressively increases from wakefulness to deep sleep. For the third question, reliability-aware latent fusion (RALF) combines this EEG encoder with independently pre-trained ECG and sEMG encoders, without joint multimodal pre-training. Each encoder contributes four summary tokens. A per-sample reliability score gates that modality’s contribu-tion to attention and the residual unimodal prediction, and missing modalities are masked explicitly. On SeizeIT2, with simultaneous wearable EEG, ECG, and EMG, RALF raises event sensitivity at 11 false alarms per hour from 61.4 % to 78.9 % and window-level AUPRC from 0.321 to 0.399 relative to the best EEG model. All six training variants exceed weighted-logit and concatenation fusion in AUPRC, most clearly on ECG-containing combinations. RALF also retains an AUPRC of at least 0.33 under every tested single-modality corruption. Relative to PhysioWave, the contributions are:  

> •

Token-efficient multiscale modeling. Dynamic scale folding shortens the sequence by a factor of J+1 while retaining adaptive wavelet analysis. We quantify its effects on accuracy, latency, memory, and computation.  

> •

An EEG foundation model across acquisition configu-rations. Route-specific front ends and extensible channel vocabularies let one shared 30M-parameter backbone learn from heterogeneous montages and transfer to unseen ones, including two-channel wearable EEG.  

> •

Reliability-aware multimodal prediction. RALF fuses compact modality summaries using sample-specific relia-bility estimates. We evaluate it on seizure detection under complete, missing, and corrupted inputs. 

2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 Physiological Foundation Models 

Following BERT [3] and MAE [4], masked reconstruction and predictive learning have produced transferable EEG represen-tations. BENDR [5] and Brant [6] established contrastive and predictive pre-training. BIOT tokenizes channel-wise spectral patches [7], LaBraM predicts discrete neural codes [8], EEGPT combines masked reconstruction with spatiotemporal alignment [9], and CBraMod separates spatial and temporal interactions through criss-cross attention [10]. Beyond EEG, ST-MEM [11], HuBERT-ECG [12], and ECG-FM [13] apply masked modeling or self-distillation to ECG, and large wristband datasets support sEMG models for movement and interaction [14], [15]. Generic time-series models such as MOMENT [16] and UniTS [17] pursue broader transfer. These models differ mainly in their tokenization and pre-training targets. PhysioWave-T instead addresses the interface be-tween acquisition and representation: configuration-specific processing precedes a shared token space, and multiscale analysis does not lengthen the backbone sequence. 

2.2 Multiscale Representations and Heterogeneous In-puts 

Wavelets localize signal structure in time and frequency [18], [19]. Best-basis selection [20] and coefficient shrinkage [21] adapt this representation to signal structure or noise, and neural wavelet operators make its filters trainable [22]. WaveFormer [1] introduced soft wavelet selection for sEMG, and PhysioWave [2] paired learnable wavelet analysis with frequency-guided pre-training for ECG and sEMG. However, PhysioWave allocated a separate token row to every band. PhysioWave-T retains these analysis components but folds the subbands before tokenization, so decomposition depth no longer lengthens the sequence. Generalizing classical best-basis selection, the fold replaces discrete wavelet-packet tree search with a learned, continuous scale mixture. Heterogeneous acquisition poses a separate challenge. Perceiver architectures compress variable inputs into latent arrays [23], mixture-of-experts models route tokens among specialized subnetworks [24], [25], and channel-independent designs process each channel separately [26]. In EEG, name-based embeddings identify electrodes [8], [9], graph models encode spatial adjacency [27], and LUNA [28] maps variable electrode sets to a fixed set of latent queries. PhysioWave-T selects a front end deterministically from acquisition meta-data rather than learning token-level routing. It also extends name-based identity to ECG leads and sEMG electrode sites, adding vocabulary entries during downstream adaptation. 

2.3 Multimodal Physiological Fusion 

Combining biosignals improves prediction when modalities carry complementary information, yet acquisition failures rarely affect all sensors equally. Even available modalities may differ substantially in their signal quality. Latent and bottleneck fusion restrict cross-modal interaction to compact summaries [23], [29], avoiding attention over every unimodal token. RALF follows this design and adds a sample-specific reliability estimate for each modality, supervised by corrup-tion severity, while explicit masks handle absent inputs. 3 PHYSIO WAVE -T A RCHITECTURE 

PhysioWave-T is built on a simple principle: acquisition-specific processing happens before tokenization, and repre-sentation learning happens after it. Route-specific front ends absorb differences in channel count and sampling rate, map-ping every recording into a common token space in which one Transformer per modality learns shared physiological structure. These front ends retain PhysioWave’s learnable wavelet analysis [2] but differ in two respects: they fold the wavelet scales into one signal before tokenization, and they attach an explicit identity to every channel. The first change keeps the sequence short, and the second lets the same backbone recognize sensors across configurations. Concretely, Fig. 1 traces a window X ∈ RC×T , with C

channels and T samples, through the encoder. The front end first decomposes each channel into J detail subbands and one approximation, then folds these J+1 bands back into a single signal ˜X ∈ RC×T with the shape of the input. Fixed-duration patches of ˜X are projected into tokens, to which learned channel-name embeddings and sinusoidal position embeddings are added. With S patches per channel, the Transformer receives C S tokens, whereas PhysioWave’s scale expansion produces (J+1) C S . Decomposition depth therefore enriches the content of each token without lengthen-ing the sequence, an advantage that grows with the number of channels and matters most for dense EEG arrays. The following subsections describe each stage in turn. 

3.1 Routes and Modular Front Ends 

Physiological corpora rarely share a single input format, and forcing them into one would require dropping chan-nels, padding, or resampling. PhysioWave-T instead groups recordings into routes . A route r = ( Cr , f r ) is defined by a channel count and a sampling rate, both read from recording metadata rather than predicted. Components tied to these quantities remain specific: each route has a wavelet front end Fr whose per-channel analysis filters and gates can specialize to the sensors of that configuration, and each sampling rate has a patch embedding Pf . Components that should generalize across configurations, namely the channel vocabulary, the position code, and the Transformer, are shared by all routes of a modality:

This architectural separation also governs how the en-coder adapts after pre-training. Because the front end is fully convolutional, it accepts any window whose duration is an integer multiple of the patch duration. A downstream task sampled at a pre-training rate reuses the corresponding patch embedding, and if it also matches a route’s channel count and channel-slot convention, it reuses that route’s front end as well. Channel identity. Because routes differ in which sensors occupy which input slots, each token must also carry the identity of its sensor. Each modality therefore maintains a vocabulary of sensor names, covering EEG electrodes, ECG leads, and sEMG sites on wristbands, forearm arrays, and grids. Position and backbone. A two-dimensional sinusoidal code over channel and patch indices is added to the tokens, and rotary position embeddings encode relative offsets inside each attention layer. The resulting representations Z serve pre-training, downstream prediction, and multimodal fusion alike, and the backbone and channel vocabulary are trans-ferred across all tasks of a modality. 

4 PRE -TRAINING , A DAPTATION , AND MULTIMODAL 

FUSION 

The EEG, ECG, and sEMG encoders share one pre-training objective but are trained independently, each on the corpora of its own modality. A pre-trained encoder can then be adapted to a downstream configuration or combined with the other two through reliability-aware latent fusion. 

4.1 Self-Supervised Pre-training 

Pre-training extends PhysioWave’s masked reconstruction in two ways: masking acts on the raw input, and the encoder reconstructs two complementary targets (Fig. 1(c)– (d)). Masking tokens alone would not suffice for this front end, because convolution and cross-scale fusion mix neighboring samples before tokenization, leaving hidden content accessible through adjacent features. Each window is therefore processed in two views. The clean view supplies reconstruction targets and mask-selection scores, while the online view zeros the selected raw-signal patches before the front end, so their samples never enter the online features. After tokenization, the masked positions are replaced by a learned mask token. Patch selection follows PhysioWave’s frequency-guided strategy: spectral importance e is com-puted from the summed magnitude spectrum along the clean-view token sequence and combined with uniform noise z as s = 0 .6 e + 0 .4 z. The highest-scoring 70 % of valid tokens are masked, so spectrally dominant patches are hidden most often and must be inferred from context. For ECG, the six limb leads within a temporal patch are masked together, since their linear dependencies would otherwise leak the hidden content, and precordial leads are masked individually. Unavailable channel slots are excluded from both mask selection and reconstruction scoring. Two shallow decoders per sampling rate then predict the masked patches in two spaces. 4.2 Downstream Adaptation 

A downstream task specifies its channel count, sampling rate, and sensor names. The Transformer, channel embedding table, projection, and identity gate are always transferred from the modality checkpoint. Front ends and patch embed-dings are transferred when the configuration matches a pre-training route, as for PTB-XL on the 12-lead ECG route, and are newly initialized otherwise. Mean-pooled tokens feed a two-layer prediction head, and we evaluate three adaptation modes. Fine-tune optimizes all parameters with layer-wise learning-rate decay in the Transformer. Probe freezes trans-ferred components and trains only the head and any newly introduced front-end or adapter parameters. Scratch trains the same architecture from random initialization, isolating the contribution of pre-training under each task protocol. 

4.3 Reliability-Aware Multimodal Fusion 

PhysioWave combines unimodal logits with one learned weight per modality. These weights are fixed after training, so they cannot reflect the degradation of a particular sensor in a particular window. Reliability-aware latent fusion (RALF; Fig. 6) fuses compact modality summaries and estimates their reliability for every window. An attention pool with q = 4 

learnable queries reduces each encoder’s token sequence to four summary tokens, and nf = 8 learnable fusion tokens attend to their concatenation through two cross-attention layers before being pooled and classified. The fusion module thus receives a fixed number of tokens per available modality, independent of window length and channel count, although encoder and pooling costs still scale with the input sequence. 5.2 EEG Results 

We evaluate three tasks following the exact evaluation proto-col of EEGPT [9]: five-stage sleep staging on Sleep-EDFx [49], target detection on PhysioNet-P300 [50], and error-related negativity detection on KaggleERN [51]. Table 2 compares PhysioWave-T with BENDR [5], BIOT [7], LaBraM [8], and EEGPT under the same subject-disjoint cross-validation folds, with baseline results cited directly from [9]. Fine-tuned PhysioWave-T achieves the highest value on eight of nine metrics, and the margins are largest where the baselines are weakest. On Sleep-EDFx, its Fpz-Cz and Pz-Oz derivations at 100 Hz match no pre-training route or vocabulary entry, so the front end and both channel embeddings are learned downstream. Even so, PhysioWave-T raises kappa from 0.686 to 0.737 and weighted F1 from 76.5 % to 80.9 % over EEGPT. The scratch model alone already reaches a kappa of 0.736, which indicates that the multiscale front end itself suits sleep staging, a task defined by band-limited rhythms; Section 6.3 shows the fold shifting weight toward the slowest scale in deep sleep. On KaggleERN, PhysioWave-T improves balanced accuracy by 3.3 points and kappa by 0.018 over EEGPT, the largest balanced-accuracy gain among the three tasks. On PhysioNet-P300, balanced accuracy and AUROC each rise by 2.0 points, while kappa is within 0.003 of EEGPT. The adaptation modes show that tasks draw on pre-training in different ways. On PhysioNet-P300, the frozen encoder already exceeds every baseline in balanced accuracy and AUROC, and fine-tuning adds only 0.9 points, so the pre-trained representation captures the P300 response largely as is. KaggleERN behaves differently: probing falls 3.4 points below scratch, yet fine-tuning exceeds scratch by 4.7 balanced-accuracy and 6.3 AUROC points. The pre-trained weights therefore contain information relevant to error-related negativity, but backbone adaptation is needed to expose it. On Sleep-EDFx, pre-training adds 1.4 balanced-accuracy points over scratch with nearly unchanged kappa, consistent with the strong contribution of our architecture.

Pre-training improves every ECG metric over scratch, with the largest benefit on CPSC 2018 and the smallest on Chapman–Shaoxing. CPSC 2018 gains 8.7 macro-F1 and 1.7 AUROC points, PTB-XL Super gains 4.0 and 2.6, and Chapman–Shaoxing, where scratch already reaches 98.5 % AUROC, gains 0.9 on both. Five-class PTB-XL accuracy rises by 3.4 points. The frozen encoder retains 95–96 % of fine-tuned AUROC on all three multi-label benchmarks, but its share of fine-tuned macro-F1 varies more widely: about 80 % on CPSC 2018, compared with 91 % on PTB-XL Super and 94 % on Chapman–Shaoxing. The pre-trained representation thus already orders records by diagnosis, and fine-tuning mainly improves the threshold-level decisions captured by macro-F1, most visibly on the nine-class CPSC 2018 task.

The reliability scores explain this behavior. On clean inputs, RALF assigns 0.92, 0.85, and 0.91 to EEG, ECG, and EMG, and corrupting one modality lowers only its own score, to 0.24–0.65, as expected from modality-local reliability heads. The drop tracks how much signal survives: bad channels, drift, saturation, dropout, and EMG noise, which swamps EMG’s broadband content, push reliability to 0.24–0.33, whereas noise on EEG or ECG leaves their dominant rhythms visible and lowers it only to 0.63–0.65. Predictions degrade correspondingly little. ECG noise costs 0.004 AUPRC, EMG dropout or noise leaves it essentially at the clean value of 0.399, so RALF does not rely on EMG when that signal is compromised, and AUPRC stays at or above 0.33 under every tested corruption.

Scale folding. Scale expansion feeds all J+1 bands to the backbone as separate token rows, giving the Transformer direct access to every subband at four times the sequence length. The dynamic fold scores 0.3 points higher on average with a quarter of the tokens, so compressing the scales before tokenization loses nothing that the backbone could have recovered from the longer sequence. How the scales are combined does matter, however. A static uniform fold drops the average by 1.6 points and falls 1.3 points below scale expansion, so a plain average of the subbands discards information that the expanded representation preserves. Of the 1.6-point gap between the uniform and dynamic folds, the learned synthesis filters account for 0.7 points, since replacing every gs with a delta costs that much, and the data-dependent weights α account for the remaining 0.9. Both components are therefore needed to match and then exceed scale expansion, and the full fold achieves this with a predictor shared across channels. On KaggleERN, the frozen encoder reaches only 53.7 %, below the 57.0 % of the scratch model, and balanced accuracy climbs monotonically with the number of tuned blocks: 57.9 % with two, 59.8 % with four, and 61.7 % with the full backbone. Error-related negativity is a brief, low-amplitude deflection embedded in ongoing fron-tocentral activity, and pre-training on four-second windows of clinical EEG does not isolate it on its own. The pre-trained weights nonetheless provide the better starting point, since fine-tuning from them ends 4.7 points above training from scratch. 

Channel identity. Setting the channel-encoding gate to zero in (12) lowers the average score by 1.8 points, and shuf-fling channel names lowers it by 2.1. The second number is the more informative one: the shuffled model keeps the same embedding parameters and receives spatial information that is simply wrong, and it performs worse than a model given no spatial information at all. The benefit of correct identity therefore comes from the scalp geometry the embeddings encode (Section 6.1), not from added capacity. The losses concentrate on PhysioNet-P300, where removing channel encoding costs 2.6 balanced-accuracy and 2.8 AUROC points, compared with 0.9 and 1.1 on KaggleERN. The P300 response has a well-defined parietal distribution, so knowing which electrode produced each token helps locate it; the error-related negativity is spatially more diffuse, and the task relies less on electrode identity.

6.1 Spatial Structure in Channel Embeddings 

Channel identities enter the model only as learned name embeddings ((12)), with no access to electrode coordinates. We test whether these embeddings nevertheless encode scalp geometry by relating their pairwise cosine similarities to geodesic electrode distances (Fig. 9a). For the 130 electrodes of the 10-5 system and the 128 EGI HydroCel positions, the Spearman correlations are ρ = −0.19 and −0.25 , more than twelve standard deviations from the permutation-null mean ( p < 0.001 over 2,000 draws), whereas the untrained table shows no association ( ρ = 0 .01 ). The structure is even clearer locally. The five nearest neighbors of each electrode in embedding space lie at a mean angular distance of 56 ◦ on the scalp for 10-5 names and 44 ◦ for EGI, compared with 84–88 ◦

after name shuffling, and the electrode-centered maps for Fz, Cz, Pz, Oz, T7, and T8 (Fig. 9b) show similarity concentrated around each reference site. Because volume conduction makes neighboring electrodes record overlapping mixtures of the same sources, pre-training on raw signals is enough for the vocabulary to recover scalp topology from names alone. This learned geometry also explains the ablation result in Section 5.6, where shuffling channel names costs more than removing channel encoding entirely.

6.3 Scale Allocation Across Sleep Stages 

Sleep staging offers a direct test of whether the folding weights track physiology, since sleep stages are defined largely by their dominant rhythms. We analyze the encoder fine-tuned on Sleep-EDFx over 37,902 thirty-second windows from 14 test subjects, grouping the weights by expert-annotated stage (Fig. 11a). The folding network receives only signal-derived subband statistics and is never told the stage, yet the weight of the slowest scale, a3 (below 6.25 Hz), rises steadily from wake to N3 and returns toward its wake value in REM. This trajectory mirrors the electrophysiology of sleep: slow-wave activity dominates N3, whereas REM EEG resembles the low-amplitude, mixed-frequency activity of wake. Within subjects (Fig. 11b), the wake-to-N3 increase holds in all 13 subjects with both stages on Pz-Oz (Wilcoxon 

p = 2 .4 × 10 −4) and in 10 of 13 on Fpz-Cz ( p = 0 .048 ), so the effect is consistent across individuals. The two derivations nonetheless receive different scale mixtures from the same shared network. At the window level (Fig. 11c), the slowest-scale weight correlates posi-tively with δ-band power share on Pz-Oz ( ρ = 0 .21 ) but negatively on Fpz-Cz ( ρ = −0.22 ). Fpz-Cz also records ocular activity, whose low-frequency power rises with eye movements during wake and REM, so δ-band power on this derivation is not a clean marker of slow-wave sleep. The fold follows the sleep stage on both derivations while responding differently to their raw low-frequency power, showing that it allocates scales according to what the low-frequency content represents on each derivation. 

7 DISCUSSION 

PhysioWave-T rests on one organizing principle: everything that depends on how a signal was acquired is handled before tokenization, and everything that depends on what it contains is learned afterward by a shared backbone. This is why a single 30 M-parameter EEG encoder can learn from 19-to 128-channel montages and then transfer to a two-channel wearable, and why decomposition depth becomes a choice about the content of each token instead of the length of the sequence. The analyses show that the efficiency costs nothing in physiological meaning: identically initialized front ends settle on the QRS, 100–200 Hz, and γ ranges for their modalities, the folding weights track sleep stages without stage labels, and the channel vocabulary recovers scalp topology from electrode names alone, which makes coordinates an unnecessary input and lets the same identity mechanism cover ECG leads and sEMG sites. The experiments also clarify when pre-training matters and how to use it. Its benefit is smallest where downstream data already cover the test conditions and largest under distribution shift and label scarcity, as in inter-session sEMG and KaggleERN, where a tenth of the labels match full-data training from scratch. Some tasks are served by the frozen representation, whereas subtle responses such as error-related negativity require adapting the backbone, so probing first and fine-tuning when needed is the natural recipe. For multimodal monitoring, RALF shows that per-sample reliability pays off when artifact-prone ECG must be weighed against EEG and at strict false-alarm budgets, and its scores fall in proportion to how much signal a corruption leaves. The corruptions studied here are synthetic; prospective recordings with natural sensor failures, joint multimodal pre-training, and new routes for modalities such as photoplethysmography are the next steps. 

8 CONCLUSION 

PhysioWave-T shows that a biosignal foundation model can accommodate heterogeneous acquisition without paying for it in sequence length. Route-specific wavelet front ends and name-based channel embeddings let one backbone per modality learn from any sensor configuration, dynamic scale folding keeps multiscale analysis within C S tokens, and the resulting EEG, ECG, and sEMG encoders lead the compared models across sleep staging, event-related potentials, cardiac diagnosis, and gesture recognition while cutting Transformer memory by up to an order of magnitude. Reliability-aware fusion extends them to multimodal seizure detection under missing and corrupted inputs, and new acquisition setups attach to the released backbones with a new front end instead of a new model.
