## Abstract 

Electroencephalography (EEG) foundation models are pre-trained on thousands of hours of recordings, yet their frozen features transfer poorly to downstream tasks, and this trans-fer does not consistently improve with larger models or more pretraining data. We show that a vision foundation model that has never seen EEG is a stronger frozen EEG encoder than these specialized models, once the signals are rendered as images that a vision transformer can read. Our method, SeeEEG, keeps the vision backbone frozen and learns only how EEG is displayed to it. Each channel occupies a ded-icated row of patches and is drawn either as a waveform at a fixed gain or as a scalogram whose frequency range spans the patch height, so that task-relevant patterns fall

## 1. Introduction 

Electroencephalography (EEG) foundation models [7, 15, 17, 40, 41, 45, 49] are pretrained on large collections of un-labeled recordings, aiming to learn representations general enough that downstream tasks require only a lightweight classifier atop the frozen encoder to achieve strong perfor-mance. Acquiring such representations demands substantial data, and most models in Tab. 1 are pretrained on thou-sands of hours of EEG recordings. Even at this scale, recent benchmarks show that their frozen features transfer poorly to many downstream tasks [21, 43, 44, 46] and do not reli-ably improve with increased model capacity or additional pretraining data [18, 21, 44, 46]. These findings suggest that learning broadly transferable frozen EEG representations remains an open challenge. Vision foundation models are trained at an even larger scale. DINOv3 [35], for example, is pretrained on 1.7 bil-lion images, and its frozen features outperform specialized models across a broad range of vision tasks. Models of this kind encode a strong prior for recognizing visual patterns. This prior is directly relevant to EEG analysis, because neu-rologists interpret EEG recordings primarily through visual inspection, recognizing spikes, periodic discharges, spindles, and artifacts by their waveforms on a page of traces. Prior work has applied frozen vision models to time series ren-dered as images for classification [32] and forecasting [6]. This cross-modal transfer aligns with the Platonic representa-tion hypothesis [13], which posits that models trained across different modalities converge toward increasingly similar representations as they scale, because images, text, and other modalities are all observations of the same underlying world. Recent theoretical work proves this convergence for joint-embedding predictive architectures under idealized condi-tions [4]. These observations motivate our central question: 

can a vision foundation model, pretrained on far more data than EEG foundation models and already proven across a wide range of visual tasks, serve as a general encoder for EEG with a learnable input module that adapts EEG signals to the frozen model? 

Existing image-based approaches for EEG either still require training on EEG data or process each channel in iso-lation. MTDP [19] probes frozen DINOv3 features extracted from the raw channel-by-time signal matrix rendered as a grayscale image, yet its final encoder is distilled from DI-NOv3 and pretrained on EEG recordings. BraVista, EEGiT, and VIPEEGNet fine-tune their vision encoders on EEG im-ages [3, 37, 48], whereas TiViT [32] processes each channel as a separate image, preventing spatial relationships across electrodes from appearing within a single image. In contrast, we keep the vision foundation model completely frozen, without any EEG pretraining, distillation, or fine-tuning. We enable it to interpret EEG signals only by learning how EEG is displayed to the backbone, together with a lightweight task head. We term this approach SeeEEG and outline it in Figs. 1 and 2. Its design follows how a ViT processes an image: the local content of each patch is embedded directly, whereas relationships between patches are captured only through attention tuned to natural images. SeeEEG therefore ren-ders each channel in a dedicated strip of patches, either as grayscale amplitude at a fixed gain or as a scalogram whose frequency range spans the patch height, so that task-relevant patterns fall within individual tokens. The content of these strips is set by a display module parameterized by the mon-tage, filtering, and contrast settings of a clinical EEG viewer, in which a bipolar or Laplacian montage places the potential difference between neighboring electrodes within a single to-ken. Initialized from a standard clinical montage, the display module is optimized with the task loss through the frozen backbone, using at most 1,113 trainable parameters per view. We evaluate SeeEEG against seven EEG foundation mod-els on 8 datasets, with all encoders frozen and paired with identical readout heads on the same samples. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • Learning the display through a frozen vision founda-tion model. We treat the montage, filtering, and contrast settings of a clinical EEG viewer as learnable inputs of a frozen ViT and optimize them with the task loss, so the learned display remains a valid page of EEG traces. • Token-local rendering. We render each channel in a dedicated row of tokens, so every token holds one channel over 16 samples, either as amplitude at a fixed gain or as its spectrum from 1 to 64 Hz. • Vision pretraining in place of EEG pretraining. Across 8 datasets, a frozen DINOv3 ViT-B outperforms every EEG foundation model in balanced accuracy and achieves a mean rank of 1.3, compared to 2.9 for the strongest baseline, REVE. The learned displays transfer to V-JEPA 2.1 and a supervised ViT. 2. Related work 

EEG foundation models. EEG foundation models are transformers pretrained on large collections of unlabeled EEG recordings, differing primarily in their pretraining objectives and in how they tokenize channels and time. BENDR learns contrastive representations of raw EEG [17], BIOT tokenizes each channel independently [45], LaBraM predicts the codes of a learned neural tokenizer for masked patches [15], EEGPT combines masked reconstruction with spatio-temporal alignment [40], CBraMod applies criss-cross attention to masked patches [41], CSBrain models EEG across temporal and spatial scales [49], and REVE pretrains on arbitrary electrode layouts using 4D positional encodings [7]. All seven models learn their representations exclusively from EEG data. Benchmarks evaluating these models under unified protocols show that the frozen fea-tures of most models transfer poorly to many downstream tasks [21, 43, 44, 46] and cannot yet serve as general-purpose fixed feature extractors [21, 46]. Neither larger model capac-ities nor additional pretraining data reliably improve down-stream performance [18, 21, 44, 46]. 

Vision foundation models for signals and EEG. The frozen features of vision foundation models transfer effec-tively to downstream tasks via a linear probe [29, 30, 35] and also generalize to non-visual signals rendered as images. AST and ViTST fine-tune pretrained vision transformers on audio spectrograms and line graphs of irregularly sam-pled time series, respectively [10, 20]. VisionTS performs zero-shot forecasting using an image-pretrained masked au-toencoder that processes time series rendered as images [6], whereas TiViT feeds each channel into a frozen ViT as a separate image [32]. In the context of EEG, MTDP probes frozen DINOv3 features extracted from the signal matrix and distills them into an encoder pretrained on EEG [19], while BraVista, EEGiT, and VIPEEGNet fine-tune vision encoders directly on EEG images [3, 37, 48]. Across all these approaches, the encoder is either trained on the target signal modality or restricted to processing each channel as a separate image. SeeEEG keeps the vision foundation model completely frozen, without any EEG pretraining, distillation, or fine-tuning. All channels are rendered within a single image, one channel per row of tokens, and SeeEEG learns only how EEG signals are displayed to the backbone through the montage, filtering, and contrast settings of a clinical EEG viewer, alongside a lightweight task head.

For a frozen ViT, a bipolar or Laplacian derivation places the potential difference between neighboring electrodes within a single strip and thus within individual tokens. We fit two such displays in closed form on the training windows of the clinical montage selected on validation. The spatial-pattern display renders the one-versus-rest common spatial patterns of the classes [5, 31], and the whitened display applies ZCA whitening [16] across channels, which decorrelates them while keeping each strip dominated by its own channel. EEG foundation models that rely on electrode identity embeddings cannot read such displays. 3.3. Readout and display fitting 

After each block b of f , we apply the final layer normaliza-tion and concatenate the class token with the mean patch token, zb = [ cls b; patch b ]. We use two readout heads, a linear probe on a single block and a token MLP that projects each of the L block vectors to 128 dimensions and classifies their concatenation through one hidden layer. Each head reads the value view, the scalogram view, or both, which gives six candidate heads per display. Validation selects the block, regularization strength, readout head, and display vari-ant by Cohen κ for multi-class tasks and AUROC for binary tasks. Optimizing the display through these readout heads in-vites two shortcuts. A display trained jointly with a head on the same windows can fit subject-specific traits, because when the label is a subject-level property, the signature of each subject predicts it. A display optimized through a fixed head can instead adapt to that head, for instance by rescaling the feature dimensions the head weights most. To prevent both shortcuts, we cross-fit the display against co-evolving readout heads. The selected learned display is then frozen and enters the final display selection as one more candidate beside the clinical and closed-form displays. 

## 4. Experimental setup 

Datasets. Our evaluation spans eight datasets covering event-type classification on TUEV [11], abnormal-EEG de-tection on TUAB [22, 24, 27], sleep staging on HMC [1, 2, 8], dementia classification on ADFTD [25], slowing classi-fication on TUSL [39], emotion recognition on SEED [47], imagined speech recognition on BCIC2020-3 [14], and mo-tor imagery classification on PhysioMI [8, 33, 34]. For BCIC2020-3, we retain the official competition split. All re-maining datasets follow the preprocessing pipeline, window-ing configurations, and subject splits established by Xiong 

et al . [44], except that we repartition the validation and test subjects for HMC. All windows are sampled at 200 Hz. 

Baselines. We compare SeeEEG against seven EEG foun-dation models: BENDR [17], BIOT [45], LaBraM [15], EEGPT [40], CBraMod [41], CSBrain [49], and REVE [7]. Each model is loaded with its released weights through the input adapters of Xiong et al . [44] and kept frozen. After each transformer block, output tokens are mean- and max-pooled and passed to our two readout heads with the same hyperparameter grids, random seeds, and validation criteria as SeeEEG. Validation also selects the montage of each base-line among those compatible with its architecture. REVE, EEGPT, and LaBraM are evaluated on the stored referen-tial, common-average, and Laplacian montages. CBraMod, which does not embed channel identities, also supports the bipolar montage and the spatial-pattern and whitened dis-plays. BENDR and BIOT use their pretraining montages, and CSBrain uses the stored referential montage.

## 5. Results 

5.1. Comparison with EEG foundation models

Rendering EEG as a standard page of traces, as in a conventional EEG viewer, yields a mean balanced accuracy of 50.5, and rendering it as a channel-by-time signal matrix [19] yields 55.8. Both fall short of the 60.0 obtained by the best EEG foundation model on each dataset. This gap is consistent with the de-sign of MTDP [19], which extracts frozen DINOv3 features from the signal matrix but still distills them into an encoder pretrained on additional EEG data. Token-local strips on the stored referential montage raise the mean to 56.8, and select-ing the best clinical montage on validation raises it to 61.6, which already lets the frozen ViT outperform every EEG foundation model on 6 of the eight datasets. The learned display raises the mean to 63.9, and the complete SeeEEG pipeline reaches 64.6. 5.3. Learned display 

Figure 3 shows how the learned display changes the input im-age, with motor imagery in the top row. Before optimization, the 16 pixel rows of a value strip replicate one waveform, so each token sees 16 copies of the same trace. After op-timization, each pixel row passes its channel through its own filter, and a single token represents the channel through 16 distinct filters. C3, Cz, and C4 lie over the hand and foot regions of the motor cortex. Starting from the stored referential montage, the display turns these electrodes into local derivations whose neighboring weights sum to +0 .90 ,

+0 .97 , and +0 .72 , respectively, close to a surface Laplacian. It also averages most lateral and temporal electrodes with their neighbors and amplifies the overall contrast by a factor of 2.2. The frozen tokens of the learned display carry more task-relevant information. On the validation windows, a lin-ear probe on the tokens of a single channel improves on all 64 channels. Its median κ rises from 15.8 to 25.8 overall and from 16.0 to 36.0 for left- versus right-hand imagery, and a probe on the mean token over all channels rises from 15.0 to 25.6. Emotion recognition, in the second row, shows the same pattern, with more class information in the frozen tokens of 54 of the 60 channels. No electrodes, frequency bands, or filters were specified in advance, and the display found these configurations through the frozen backbone from task labels alone.

A randomly initialized ViT-B with the same display and readout heads scores 15.2 points below DINOv3 on average and up to 36.6 points below it on slowing clas-sification. The ability to read rendered EEG comes from natural image pretraining. Shifting the image vertically by a full token row keeps each strip aligned with the patch grid and changes the score by only -0.3 points on average. A half-row shift makes every token straddle two adjacent strips and degrades performance on 6 of the 8 datasets, by up to 29.7 points on slowing classification. The frozen ViT relies on isolating each channel within the tokens of its own strip. Two further controls change the content of individual to-kens. Cyclically shifting the 16 pixel rows of each strip by 8 rows keeps all pixel intensities and the channel and time span of every token, and changes only the row order within each patch, which is the order of scalogram frequencies and, in a learned display, of value-row filters. This permutation costs up to 33.7 points on slowing classification and also lowers dementia classification and imagined speech recog-nition. On these three tasks, whose discriminative evidence lies in the spectral profile, the frozen ViT reads the spectrum from its spatial layout within each token, much as it reads local texture in an image patch. Shuffling the time columns keeps the marginal power at every frequency but destroys temporal order. This perturbation degrades performance on 6 of the 8 datasets and by 6.3 points on average, so SeeEEG exploits temporal dynamics beyond the static spectrum.

## 6. Conclusion 

A vision foundation model with no prior exposure to EEG can serve as a stronger frozen EEG encoder than specialized EEG foundation models, provided that each channel is ren-dered within a dedicated row of tokens and the display is learned through the frozen backbone. Consistent with the Platonic representation hypothesis [13], this indicates that much of the structure that EEG foundation models aim to learn from scarce recordings is already encoded in models pretrained on natural images, and that adapting to a new EEG task primarily requires learning an appropriate display. EEG representation learning can benefit directly from future advances in vision foundation models, since a new back-bone can be adapted to EEG by refitting only the lightweight display and readout head. This principle readily extends to other multichannel physiological signals that domain ex-perts interpret through visual inspection, such as MEG and ECG.
