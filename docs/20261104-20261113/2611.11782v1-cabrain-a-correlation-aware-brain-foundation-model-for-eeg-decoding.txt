Abstract 

Electroencephalography (EEG) foundation models have emerged as promis-ing paradigms for decoding brain activity through large-scale self-supervised pretraining. Notably, most existing approaches employ masked patch recon-struction, in which randomly selected EEG patches are masked and recovered by the network. However, purely random masking may limit representation learning as informative patches from highly correlated channels often remain unmasked, causing the network to underutilize weakly correlated channels or serial dependencies. Furthermore, because these models are typically based on transformer architectures, their performance is influenced by the choice of positional encoding. Although positional encoding has been extensively explored in natural language processing and computer vision, few studies have adapted it to the unique characteristics of EEG signals. To address these limitations, this study introduces a conditional positional encoding method based on graph convolutional networks (GCNs) to explicitly model inter-channel correlations, along with an adaptive masking strategy that en-hances representation learning by modulating the difficulty of masked patch reconstruction. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to inte-grate graph-based representations into conditional positional encoding and introduce adaptive masking within EEG foundation model pretraining. 1. Introduction 

Electroencephalography (EEG) is a fundamental sensing modality for brain–computer interfaces and healthcare applications, offering millisecond-level measurements of brain activity in a noninvasive and cost-effective man-ner. EEG signal analysis has been widely applied across various domains and has demonstrated substantial value. Representative application areas of EEG signal analysis include (1) diagnosis and monitoring of neurological disorders, such as epileptic seizure detection [1] and sleep stage classification [2, 3]; (2) brain–computer interfaces based on motor imagery [4, 5] or imag-ined speech classification [6]; (3) cognitive and affective state assessment, including attention [7], stress [8, 9], and emotion recognition [10, 11]. Various machine learning models have been proposed for EEG signal anal-ysis, evolving from approaches based on handcrafted features [12, 13] to ad-vanced deep learning methods, such as convolutional neural network (CNN) [14, 15, 16], graph convolutional network (GCN) [16], long short-term mem-ory [17, 18], transformers [19, 20], and diffusion models [21]. Despite these technological advancements, learning robust and transferable EEG represen-tations remains challenging because of (1) the low signal-to-noise ratio of EEG signals, (2) substantial inter-subject and inter-session variability, (3) heterogeneous channel configurations and reference schemes, and (4) varia-tions in sampling rates and sequence lengths. These challenges hinder out-of-distribution generalization and cross-dataset transfer of models developed to decode brain activity. Moreover, the limited availability of large-scale annotated datasets presents an additional obstacle to achieving reliable per-formance. To mitigate these challenges, recent research has increasingly explored large-scale self-supervised learning (SSL), a paradigm that has transformed fields such as natural language processing and computer vision. Using SSL, EEG encoders can learn generalizable and transferable feature representa-tions, reducing their reliance on extensively labeled datasets. patch reconstruction has been widely employed as a pretext task for the self-supervised pretraining of EEG foundation models. In this technique, a subset of EEG patches is randomly masked, and the neural network is trained to reconstruct the missing patches from the remaining context. A series of representative EEG foundation models, including BENDR [22], BIOT [23], EEG2Rep [35], LaBraM [37], EEGPT [34], CBraMod [29], and EEGMamba [30], have focused on the segmentation of EEG signals into patches, encoding of these patches, development of more efficient attention mechanisms, inte-gration of SSL strategies to obtain generic representations from EEG signals, and incorporation of positional information of EEG patches. Among the aforementioned frameworks, CBraMod [29] is notable for its evaluation on more than ten heterogeneous test datasets, where it achieved state-of-the-art performance at the time of publication. One of the main 3contributions of CBraMod is the development of asymmetric conditional po-sitional encoding (ACPE). Conditional positional encoding (CPE) [45] was originally proposed for vision transformers. Unlike conventional fixed posi-tional encodings, CPE is dynamically computed from the input data, en-abling greater adaptability across diverse datasets. In CBraMod, the raw EEG signal from each channel is segmented into small chunks of prede-termined duration, and each chunk is defined as an EEG patch. A two-dimensional (2D) grid is constructed by stacking patches from different chan-nels in an arbitrary order, with each patch representing a single element. ACPE is applied to this grid representation, enabling convolutional opera-tions to treat EEG patches analogously to image patches. A key limitation of ACPE is that it treats the constructed grid repre-sentation as an image and applies convolution. While pixels within local neighborhoods are strongly correlated owing to spatial continuity in images, EEG grid representations do not necessarily ensure strong correlations be-tween adjacent patches. Figure 1 illustrates this limitation. Specifically, Figure 1(a) highlights the channels strongly correlated with Cz, whereas Fig-ure 1(b) shows that most of these correlated channels lie outside the recep-tive field of the convolutional kernel in the stacked 2D tensor representation. The channel correlations are estimated using the Temple University Hospital EEG Corpus (TUEG) [46] dataset, which is also used as the training dataset in this study. Consequently, convolution may aggregate information from weakly correlated channels while overlooking more informative but spatially distant channels in the stacked 2D tensor representation. Thus, the posi-tional embeddings may not accurately reflect the underlying inter-channel relationships, reducing their effectiveness as positional cues for EEG repre-sentation learning. In contrast, graph convolution operates on neighborhoods defined by inter-channel correlations rather than arbitrary spatial proximity, enabling positional embeddings to be generated from more meaningful local relationships. Motivated by this observation, we propose a novel GCN-based CPE ap-proach. Additionally, we examine the effectiveness of SSL approaches based on masked patch reconstruction in capturing the underlying structures of EEG signals. The conventional reconstruction of randomly masked patches involves hiding certain patches at random and training the model to recover them using the surrounding context. However, random masking inherently limits representation learning because informative signals, such as patches from strongly correlated channels, may remain unmasked. In the random 4masking example shown in Figure 1(b), patches from channels strongly cor-related with Cz are not masked simultaneously within the same time slot. Even in the most challenging case (time slot 1), one strongly correlated chan-nel (Pz) remains observable. Across the 30 time slots shown in the example, an average of 2.8 strongly correlated channels remain unmasked per time slot. Consequently, highly informative correlated-channel signals remain available for reconstructing masked Cz patches. To enhance the representation learn-ing capability of neural networks, information from weakly correlated chan-nels and serial correlations must be considered. However, the consistent availability of information from strongly correlated channels may prompt neural networks to underutilize weakly correlated channels or serial corre-lations during pretraining. To address this, we introduce a novel adaptive masking strategy for SSL that adjusts the difficulty of masked patch recon-struction, encouraging the utilization of information from weakly correlated channels and serial correlations. Both proposed methods, i.e., GCN-based CPE and adaptive masking, leverage the correlation information inherent in EEG signals. Unlike abso-lute measures like the number or arrangement of EEG electrodes and signal amplitude, correlation is less affected by dataset variability. The key contri-butions of this work can be summarized as follows. • GCN-based CPE. We introduce a CPE method based on a GCN, in which graph edges are determined by channel correlations. By per-forming convolution exclusively on strongly correlated channels, the proposed positional encoding effectively integrates local correlation, capturing more invariant features and improving adaptability to unseen EEG electrode configurations during foundation model pretraining. • Adaptive masking strategy for effective representation learn-ing. We propose a novel adaptive masking strategy for SSL that affords control over the reconstruction difficulty of masked patches and poten-tially facilitates a deeper understanding of EEG signals. While adaptive masking has been extensively studied in large language models (LLMs) and computer vision, to the best of our knowledge, this study repre-sents the first attempt at applying adaptive masking for self-supervised pretraining of EEG foundation models. • Comprehensive experiments on diverse datasets. Each proposed approach achieves performance comparable or superior to that of re-5cent state-of-the-art frameworks. The combination of both methods results in performance superior to that achieved by either approach individually. 

2. Related work 

2.1. EEG Foundation Model 

EEG foundation models differ in several aspects, including backbone architecture and pretraining objective. From an architectural perspective, transformer-based models have been widely adopted, although recent stud-ies have also explored state-space models as an alternative backbone archi-tecture. However, architectural differences alone do not fully explain the methodological diversity of recent EEG foundation models, as models with similar architectures often adopt different self-supervised learning objectives. In contrast, pretraining objectives determine what information the model is encouraged to learn from unlabeled EEG signals and have thus evolved along several directions. Therefore, the following review categorizes EEG foundation models according to their pretraining objectives. Based on pretraining objectives, existing EEG foundation models can be broadly classified into three groups: contrastive learning, autoregressive prediction, and signal reconstruction-based pretraining. Contrastive learn-ing methods aim to learn invariant EEG representations by maximizing the agreement between related samples while separating unrelated samples. Rep-resentative examples include BENDR [22] and BIOT [23]. Autoregressive prediction follows a causal next-token prediction paradigm, wherein future EEG tokens are predicted from preceding context using causal masking, sim-ilar to the training of LLMs. Representative examples include Neuro-GPT [24], NeuroLM [25], BrainGPT [26], and THD-BAR [27]. Among these three groups, signal reconstruction-based pretraining has emerged as the domi-nant paradigm. In signal reconstruction-based pretraining, a portion of the input is masked and the model is trained to recover the missing informa-tion from the remaining context. Depending on the reconstruction target, existing approaches can be further divided into three categories: raw-signal reconstruction, representation reconstruction, and frequency-domain recon-struction. The first category reconstructs the raw EEG signal. Models such as FoME [28], CBraMod [29], EEGMamba [30], CSBrain [31], REVE [32], and LUNA [33] learn to recover masked EEG segments using reconstruction losses 6Figure 2: General framework of raw-signal reconstruction-based EEG foundation models. 

applied to the original signal. Unlike representation-based objectives, raw-signal reconstruction operates on the original EEG measurements and en-courages the preservation of information contained in the input signal. The second category reconstructs intermediate EEG representations rather than the original signal itself. Depending on the representation space, the re-construction target may consist of continuous latent embeddings or discrete codebook representations. In embedding reconstruction, EEG patches are transformed into latent representations using a representation encoder, and the model is trained to predict the masked latent features. Representative models include EEGPT [34], EEG2Rep [35], and MIRepNet [36]. In contrast, codebook-based approaches first map EEG representations into discrete code-book entries through vector quantization and then predict the corresponding codebook indices. Representative examples include LaBraM [37], BrainOmni [38], EpilepsyFM [39], and CodeBrain [40]. By defining the reconstruction target in a learned representation space rather than the original signal do-main, these approaches encourage the model to focus on higher-level EEG characteristics while reducing the influence of low-level signal variability. The third category focuses on reconstructing frequency-domain informa-tion, where the reconstruction target is formulated using spectral representa-tions instead of time-domain signals or learned representations. BrainBERT 7[41], EEGFormer [42], BrainWave [43], and Uni-NTFM [44] are representative examples. Because spectral characteristics play an important role in many EEG decoding tasks, frequency-domain objectives encourage the model to capture oscillatory patterns that may not be readily observable in the time domain. Although reconstruction targets differ, many EEG foundation models adopt a broadly similar pretraining pipeline, as illustrated in Figure 2. Con-tinuous EEG recordings are first segmented into fixed-length temporal patches for each channel. These patches are then transformed into feature represen-tations using temporal, spectral, or hybrid encoders. After positional in-formation is incorporated, the resulting patch embeddings are processed by a backbone network, typically a transformer encoder. The model is then trained to infer masked patches from the surrounding context according to the chosen reconstruction target. 

2.2. Positional Encoding 

In natural language processing and computer vision, positional encoding plays a critical role in enabling transformer-based models to capture the order and spatial structure of the input data. The most commonly used approach is absolute positional encoding, in which fixed or learnable vectors are assigned to specific positions within an input sequence or image grid. This strategy, first popularized in natural language processing, allows the model to differentiate between tokens and patches based on their absolute locations. In contrast, relative positional encoding represents the distance or offset between tokens or patches, enabling translational invariance. Recently, CPE [45] has been proposed as a positional encoding scheme in which encodings are dynamically generated based on the local neighbor-hood of input tokens rather than being fixed or predefined. CPE is typically implemented using a positional encoding generator, often using lightweight 2D convolutions, making it efficient and easy to integrate into transformer architectures. In contrast to absolute or relative encodings, CPE can natu-rally generalize to input sequences that are longer than those observed during training. Originally introduced for vision tasks, CPE was designed to satisfy the following three desirable properties: • exhibit permutation variance while preserving translation equivariance • generalize to sequences longer than those encountered during training 8• retain a certain degree of absolute positional information to ensure strong performance Furthermore, Saijo and Ogawa [48] referred to CPE as “no positional encod-ing” and reported that it achieved favorable performance on speech separa-tion datasets involving sequences longer than those used during pretraining. 

2.3. Masking Strategies in SSL 

In natural language processing, SSL has been widely implemented using masked language modeling (MLM). Motivated by this approach, masked im-age modeling (MIM) has been extensively explored in computer vision. MLM predicts missing words in a text sequence based on the surrounding context, whereas MIM reconstructs the masked regions in an image by leveraging the visible information. Although random masking has been predominantly used in both MLM [49, 50] and MIM [51, 52, 53], adaptive masking has received increasing attention owing to its potential to improve the represen-tation learning capacity. Recent work on MLM [54] has shown that not all masked tokens require the same degree of reasoning for prediction, and that logical words, such as “more”, “and”, and “hence” entail more complex reasoning than ordinary words. Experiments demonstrated that preferentially masking logical words can foster more effective reasoning than random masking. Kohli et al. [55] proposed a domain-specific masking approach in which tokens associated with specific concepts are selectively masked to enable continual learning with reduced training overhead. Among recent MIM research, Feng and Zhang [56] demonstrated that fixed masking patterns constrain the modeling of visual cues. Therefore, the authors proposed an evolved part-based masking method using an adaptive part-partition module that enables more general visual cue modeling. In the work of Bandara et al. [57], a categorical distribution was estimated over space–time-patch tokens and those expected to yield larger reconstruction errors were rewarded and chosen as visible tokens. Shah et al. [58] intro-duced a pretraining approach for cataract surgery video analysis, where token selection and masking were guided by spatiotemporal importance instead of random choice. Dai et al. [35] argued that the absence of structure in randomly masked data hinders the learning of semantic representations. The authors proposed 9the semantic subsequence preserving method, which focuses on selecting re-gions to preserve rather than regions to mask. However, as the selection of preserved regions is random, the method remains closer to random masking than adaptive masking approaches used in LLMs and computer vision. More recently, Tang et al. [39] proposed a brain-region masking strategy in which groups of channels corresponding to predefined anatomical brain regions are masked. By removing spatially related channels together, the model is encouraged to infer missing information from inter-regional depen-dencies rather than relying on isolated channels. Unlike random masking, this approach explicitly incorporates domain knowledge regarding brain or-ganization into the masking process. 

3. Method 

This section describes the proposed framework, CABrain, incorporating novel positional encoding and mask generation methods within the pre-training framework of an EEG foundation model. First, a brief overview of CABrain is provided, and Subsections 3.1 and 3.2 outline the positional encoding and masking strategies, respectively. The EEG signal from each channel is segmented into one-second intervals. Assuming a sampling frequency of 200 Hz, each one-second patch consists of 200 data points. A predetermined percentage of patches is masked, and the corresponding feature vectors are replaced with zeros. Unlike prior studies that predominantly relied on random masking, the proposed approach adopts an adaptive mask generation strategy designed to enhance the representa-tion learning capability. After masking, patch encoding proceeds through two pathways: A temporal pathway applies one-dimensional CNNs to ex-tract time-domain features, whereas a spectral pathway applies a fast Fourier transform for frequency-domain feature extraction. The two types of features are merged via element-wise summation. Positional embeddings computed using the proposed method are added to yield a patch embedding for the transformer encoder. Let C denote the number of EEG channels and N be the number of patches per channel. Each EEG patch is denoted by x ∈ Rℓ,and the set of patches is X = { xi,j | i ∈ [1 , 2, . . . , C ], j ∈ [1 , 2, . . . , N ]}.The EEG patches are first processed by the transformer encoder through self-attention, and subsequently passed to a masked EEG patch reconstruc-tion head. 3.1. GCN-Based CPE 

CPE [45] uses CNNs to generate positional embeddings conditioned within a local spatiotemporal context. Based on this concept, CBraMod [29] intro-duced ACPE, in which per-channel EEG signals are stacked into a 2D matrix (with arbitrary channel ordering) and convolved using an asymmetric 19×7 kernel. The patch grid is zero-padded by nine elements on the top and bot-tom, and by three elements on the left and right. Figure 3 illustrates the convolutional filter used to compute the positional embedding for the third patch of the Cz channel. Channels whose correlation with Cz is ≥ 0.5 are highlighted in purple. Although CBraMod with ACPE achieves state-of-the-art results for a range of downstream tasks, it exhibits two limitations. First, convolution on a 2D tensor obtained from arbitrary channel stack-ing may not reliably capture informative local structures. Positional en-coding is essential in transformer architectures because self-attention alone is permutation-invariant and cannot distinguish the positions of input to-kens. Therefore, positional embeddings should provide location-dependent cues that enable the model to differentiate one token from another while pre-serving meaningful structural relationships. CPE [45] was originally designed for images, which typically exhibit strong local pixel correlations. The corre-lation coefficient analysis in [59] indicated that adjacent pixels in images are 11 sufficiently strongly correlated such that the values of neighboring pixels can be largely inferred from a single pixel. Because positional embeddings are computed by convolving neighboring pixels, these strong local correlations allow the convolution operation to produce stable and consistent positional cues that reflect the underlying spatial structure of the image. However, as shown in Figure 3, convolution over the 2D tensor formed by arbitrarily stacking EEG channels leverages only two channels whose correlation with Cz is ≥ 0.5. The remaining lower-correlation channels may provide little in-formative signals, potentially introducing noisy information. Consequently, the convolution may generate inconsistent positional embeddings that do not capture the underlying correlation structure of EEG channels, limiting their effectiveness as positional cues for transformer-based EEG representation learning. Second, zero padding along the boundaries may leak nontrivial cues about the absolute position rather than only weak signals. Among the three re-quirements identified in [45] for effective positional encoding in vision tasks, the third is the capacity to slightly convey the absolute positions. In con-ventional vision tasks, boundary padding occupies only a small fraction of the receptive field, such that convolution outputs are still dominated by the underlying image content and padding provides only weak positional cues. In contrast, the ACPE employed in CBraMod pads the EEG grid by nine elements vertically and three elements horizontally for a 19×7 convolution. Thus, a large portion of the receptive field for boundary patches may con-sist of padded zeros. In this case, the convolution output may be influenced more strongly by the padding pattern than by the EEG patches themselves, causing positional embeddings to encode excessive absolute positional infor-mation rather than only weak boundary cues, deviating from the original design objective of CPE. To address the first issue, in this study, a graph is constructed using EEG electrodes as nodes, and a GCN-based CPE strategy is adopted. To address the second issue, we reconsider whether absolute positional information is necessary for EEG representation learning. Among the requirements for ef-fective CPE [45], the condition that positional encodings provide absolute position “to a certain degree” appears to be motivated primarily by em-pirical observations, with limited theoretical justification. While positional encodings in natural language processing represent token locations within a sequence, EEG electrodes represented as graph nodes are characterized by their topological relationships rather than by a well-defined absolute position. 12 Consequently, introducing absolute positional cues may offer limited gains in EEG graphs and may even promote overfitting to dataset-specific electrode layouts. Therefore, the proposed GCN-based CPE is designed without ex-plicit absolute positional information, relying solely on structural relation-ships among electrodes. This design preserves generalization performance while avoiding the excessive positional bias introduced by zero padding. Representing EEG data as a graph for GCNs requires the definition of two components: node features and an adjacency matrix. EEG channels are defined as nodes, whereas the patch embedding serves as a node feature. For datasets with different channel configurations, graphs are reconstructed using the channels available in each dataset rather than being mapped to a common channel subset. Despite differences in the numbers of nodes and edge connections, the pretrained GCN weights remain applicable because graph convolution is defined through shared neighborhood aggregation and weight matrices that are independent of the specific graph topology. As GCN parameters operate on feature dimensions rather than individual node identities, the same pretrained weights can be directly applied to graphs with different numbers of nodes (e.g., 62 or 6 channels) and connectivity patterns. For an input represented as a C × N matrix, N graphs are constructed to compute the positional embeddings for each time slot. Notably, an adja-cency matrix encoding inter-channel relations can be constructed using either distance-based or correlation-based methods. The distance-based method ex-ploits the physical geometry of EEG electrodes. A method for constructing a distance-based adjacency matrix can be found in [60]. However, distance-based methods have two limitations. First, they can only be applied when feature values are generated by encoding signals from individual channels and cannot be readily employed when bipolar montage channels are used. Because bipolar montage channels represent voltage differences between elec-trode pairs, a meaningful physical distance between bipolar channels cannot be defined. Second, the physical locations of electrodes may vary across in-dividuals and recording systems owing to differences in head size, electrode placement, and acquisition protocols. Moreover, even when subjects perform the same task, the spatial distribution of EEG responses may vary across individuals, resulting in different sets of channels carrying the most informa-tive signals. Consequently, physically adjacent electrodes do not necessarily exhibit similar functional roles across individuals. Such inter-subject variabil-ity can lead to inconsistencies in distance-based graph structures and may reduce the robustness and generalization capability of positional encodings 13 Figure 4: Graph convolution with edges defined by the top three inter-channel correlations. 

across heterogeneous datasets. Considering these aspects, this study adopts a correlation-based method for constructing an adjacency matrix. Unlike distance-based graph construc-tion methods that rely on electrode coordinates, the proposed approach con-structs graphs solely from inter-channel correlation statistics. Therefore, the graph structure is independent of the physical arrangement and total number of electrodes, making it applicable to datasets with heterogeneous channel configurations. The absolute value of the Pearson correlation coefficient be-tween node features was used to determine graph connectivity. The Pear-son correlation coefficients are estimated from the TUEG [46] dataset during foundation model pretraining. A separate set of coefficients is estimated using only the training split of each target dataset during downstream fine-tuning. In general, graph connectivity can be determined using various strategies, in-cluding threshold-based edge selection and top-K neighbor selection. In this study, we adopt the latter approach, where each node is connected to its K most strongly correlated neighbors together with the self-connections. Var-ious prior studies have similarly defined graph structures using correlations between nodes [60, 61]. Figure 4 illustrates a simplified case in which nodes are connected to the three most strongly correlated nodes, and positional encodings are subsequently computed using a GCN. When computing positional encodings with a GCN, the patch embed-dings to be employed as node features must be obtained after the masking step. If the patch embeddings are computed from the data before masking and fed into the GCN, the resulting positional embeddings may introduce a unique challenge. When these embeddings are added to the masked patch 14 embeddings and passed through the transformer encoder for masked patch reconstruction pretraining, the GCN may encode information tailored to the reconstruction objective rather than providing meaningful positional cues. In extreme cases, the GCN may degenerate to reproduce its input patch embeddings. The transformer encoder and reconstruction head then mini-mize reconstruction loss by overfitting the training data while failing to learn useful positional representations. In contrast, when zero-filled patch em-beddings derived from masking are included as node features, making the GCN preserve as much of the input information as possible does not bene-fit the reconstruction task. Instead, the presence of zero-filled embeddings implicitly induces an edge-drop effect in GCN computation. Although the edges are not explicitly removed, multiplication with zero-filled features effec-tively cancels certain connections. As reported by Rong et al. [62], randomly dropping edges during GCN computation helps mitigate overfitting and over-smoothing. Incorporating zero-filled embeddings may thus provide a similar regularization effect. The selection of patches for masking is critical for learning transferable representations from unlabeled EEG signals. Robust and generic EEG repre-sentations can be learned from reconstruction tasks of appropriate difficulty. To design such a masking method, the information available for inferring the original data is first analyzed. Although the inference process of AI models cannot be fully determined, an approximate understanding can guide the de-velopment of masking strategies that promote more effective reasoning than random masking. To this end, both the inter-channel and serial correlations of EEG signals are exploited. Inter-channel correlation indicates the relationship between EEG channels, whereas serial correlation defines the temporal correlation within a single channel over time. As shown in Figure 5(a), for each of the 19 channels, the proportions of Pearson correlation coefficients that are greater than or equal to 0.5 and 0.3 with other channels are 37.95% and 56.79%, respectively. In other words, various channels exhibit nonnegligible correla-tions, making it unlikely that all of them are masked simultaneously during random masking. Hence, the information from moderately or strongly cor-related channels can likely be used to reconstruct randomly masked patches. 16 Figure 5(b) shows that the channel correlations are stronger among the simultaneously recorded signals, whereas those across different time slots re-main weak. This suggests that correlations of channels from different time slots are less likely to be leveraged for masked patch reconstruction. In Figure 5(c), patch colors represent the average values of the elements in the feature vector, with similar colors across consecutive patches suggesting a strong se-rial correlation. Although horizontally adjacent patches often exhibit similar colors, abrupt changes occur frequently. Therefore, relying on serial correla-tion may occasionally increase the reconstruction error, and when strongly correlated channels are available, their contribution to pretraining may be limited. In summary, Figure 5 reveals two key characteristics of EEG patch re-construction. First, strongly or moderately correlated channels are likely to remain available under random masking, causing the model to rely on these highly informative channels while underutilizing weakly correlated channels. Second, serial correlations provide useful temporal cues, albeit with reduced utility and reliability compared with strongly correlated channels. Conse-quently, random masking may not adequately encourage the model to simul-taneously exploit weakly correlated channels and temporal dependencies. To promote more robust representation learning, the masking strategy should in-tentionally reduce the availability of strongly correlated channel information while preserving sufficient temporal context for reconstruction. Therefore, the proposed masking strategy aims to create reconstruction scenarios in which weakly correlated channels and temporal dependencies become neces-sary sources of information rather than optional alternatives. The proposed kernel density estimation (KDE)-based masking strategy is designed to achieve two objectives. 1. Increasing the probability of selecting already heavily masked time slots. Even when a time slot already contains many masked patches, the proposed KDE scheme can further increase its sampling probability if neighboring time slots still contain sufficient unmasked patches. In this scenario, certain time slots become heavily masked while nearby time slots remain observable. This reduces the availability of strongly correlated channel information within the heavily masked time slots and encourages the model to rely more on weakly correlated channels for reconstruction. 2. Preventing simultaneous heavy masking of adjacent time slots. If mul-17 tiple adjacent time slots become heavily masked simultaneously, tem-poral dependencies can no longer be exploited for reconstruction. The KDE therefore decreases the probability of selecting time slots sur-rounded by heavily masked neighbors. This mechanism limits long runs of consecutive masking and preserves temporal context, increasing the likelihood that both weakly correlated channels and serial dependencies are leveraged during reconstruction. Together, these objectives are intended to create reconstruction scenarios in which strongly correlated channel information becomes insufficient while temporal context remains available. Such scenarios encourage the model to simultaneously exploit weakly correlated channels and serial dependencies, which are rarely required under conventional random masking. The proposed KDE facilitates the generation of these scenarios by incorporating the distri-bution of unmasked patches across neighboring time slots into the masking process. The proposed method first performs KDE by placing the kernels in pro-portion to the number of unmasked patches within each time slot. Based on the resulting probability distribution, a time slot is sampled, and one unmasked patch is randomly chosen from that time slot. This density es-timation and sampling procedure is iteratively performed until the desired number of masked patches is obtained. In Figure 6(a), black patches indicate unmasked regions, and numbers above the grid represent the number of un-masked patches per time slot. The notation “× K” indicates that the kernel centered at a specific time slot is accumulated K times when estimating the density. Therefore, time slots containing more unmasked patches contribute more strongly to the estimated distribution and also increase the probability of selecting neighboring time slots. When 50% of the patches are randomly masked, it is highly unlikely that a single time slot will contain 80–90% of the masked patches. This scenario occurs in the 20th and 21st slots, as shown in Figure 6(a). Replacing random sampling with a KDE-based pro-posal distribution, ˆfh(t), which incorporates neighboring time slots, increases the density around the 20th and 21st slots. This allows masking to occur at proportions well above the average in these slots. Moreover, this increase in sampling probability occurs only when neighboring time slots have sufficient unmasked patches. This configuration enhances the likelihood of exploiting both serial correlations and weakly correlated channels for reconstruction. 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experiment setup 

Pretraining dataset and preprocessing. CABrain was pretrained using the large-scale public dataset TUEG [46]. EEG recordings shorter than 5 min, along with the first and last minutes of each recording, were discarded to reduce low-quality segments. Nineteen common EEG channels from the 10–20 system were selected. The corresponding signals were band-pass filtered (0.3–75 Hz) and notch filtered (60 Hz), resampled to 200 Hz, and segmented into non-overlapping 30-second windows. Samples containing values exceeding ±100 μV were automatically removed, and all signals were normalized by 100 μV to ensure that values mainly ranged between –1 and 1. 

Pretraining. Because generating adaptive masks using nonparametric KDE is computationally expensive, 50,000 masks were generated and ran-domly sampled during pretraining. Using a single-core AMD Ryzen Thread-ripper PRO 3975WX CPU, generating 50,000 masks required approximately 5 h. If all 44,385,280 masks required for 40 epochs were to be generated dur-ing training, nearly 4,400 h would be consumed. Therefore, pre-generation and reuse of masks are crucial. The two proposed methods, GCN-based 20 CPE and adaptive masking, can be applied independently during pretraining; however, caution is required when applying them simultaneously. Adaptive masking can simultaneously mask various patches within specific time slots, increasing the number of cases in which the input nodes to the GCN have feature vectors consisting entirely of zeros. This may hinder the construc-tion of informative positional embeddings. Therefore, we first pretrained a model with adaptive masking alone and subsequently applied GCN-based CPE through transfer learning to build the foundation model. 

Test datasets. Experiments were conducted on 10 publicly available EEG datasets, as summarized in Table 1.

Metrics. To ensure a fair comparison with previous studies, the following evaluation metrics were applied: For binary classification, the performance was assessed using balanced accuracy, area under the precision–recall curve (AUC-PR), and area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC), 21 with AUROC designated as the monitoring score. Performance metrics are reported as the mean ± standard deviation over five runs with different random seeds. 

Model architecture and implementation. The proposed GCN-based CPE consisted of two GCN layers and was implemented using PyTorch Geo-metric. The input and output dimensions of the GCNConv layers were iden-tical to the patch embedding dimension, and a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function was applied between the two GCNConv layers. The non-parametric KDE for adaptive mask generation was implemented using the gaussian_kde function provided by the SciPy library. The structures of the classification heads were as follows: For the ISRUC and BCIC-IV-2a datasets, a single fully connected layer was employed; for the BCIC2020-3 and CHB-MIT datasets, two fully connected layers were set; and three fully connected layers were adopted for the remaining datasets. Binary cross-entropy loss was adopted for binary classification, whereas cross-entropy loss was used for multi-class classification.

These observations suggest a possible relationship between the number of classes and relative utility of the two proposed components, although other dataset characteristics may also contribute to the observed differences. In many binary-class EEG tasks, such as mental stress detection and mental disorder diagnosis, the primary objective is to distinguish between two brain states. Compared with multi-class EEG decoding, the distinction between classes is simpler. This aspect is reflected in Figure 8(a), where the two classes in Mumtaz2016 exhibit distinct global topographic patterns. Such coarse-grained differences can often be identified from broader temporal and inter-channel contextual information without precise modeling of localized channel-wise spatial relationships. The proposed adaptive masking strategy encourages the model to leverage weakly correlated channels and temporal dependencies during masked patch reconstruction, thereby preventing exces-sive reliance on a subset of channels. In contrast, multi-class EEG decoding generally involves more compli-cated decision boundaries, requiring finer discrimination among multiple classes. As illustrated in Figure 8(b), the four classes in BCIC-IV-2a ex-hibit more subtle and localized differences in their EEG topographies. Dis-tinguishing these classes requires preserving fine-grained spatial relationships among channels, making accurate positional information more beneficial for multi-class EEG decoding. Under these settings, preserving subtle class-specific spatial relationships among EEG channels may become critical. The 28 proposed GCN-based CPE approach explicitly preserves correlation-aware spatial topology, enabling the model to effectively exploit localized discrimi-native channel relationships. Overall, the results suggest that the relative utility of the two compo-nents may depend on the nature of the classification task. Adaptive masking appears to be particularly beneficial when coarse-grained differences between classes can be captured from broader contextual information, whereas GCN-based CPE may provide greater benefits when accurate modeling of subtle spatial relationships among EEG channels is required. The observed ten-dency may help explain why adaptive masking achieved stronger performance on several binary-class datasets, whereas GCN-based positional encoding per-formed more favorably on many multi-class datasets.

The average relative accuracies for the five node degrees are shown in Figure 9. Moderate connectivity (three edges per node) resulted in the best performance. When each node was connected to a single strongly correlated node, the performance decreased by approximately 3% relative to the average accuracy across datasets. This degradation is likely attributable to the fact that with only a single-edge connection, the model had no alternative nodes to compensate with when the signal of the connected node was corrupted 30 by noise. Furthermore, if the connected node corresponded to a masked patch, the graph effectively lost that edge, rendering the GCN computation equivalent to operating on a disconnected structure. These factors explain the sharp decline in performance when the node degree was limited to one. Additionally, when the node degree reached four or higher, the performance gradually declined. This degradation can be attributed to excessive connec-tivity, which introduces redundancy and may dilute the discriminative power of graph representation.

Therefore, a moderate kernel bandwidth (approximately 0.5) is a reasonable choice, providing a balance between under-smoothing at significantly small bandwidths and oversmoothing at larger bandwidths. 4.4. Validation of Design Choices 

To justify the major design choices of CABrain, additional comparative experiments were conducted on both the masking strategy and GCN archi-tecture. Specifically, we compare the proposed KDE-based adaptive masking strategy with several EEG-oriented masking baselines and evaluate alterna-tive uses of GCN, including a GCN-only architecture and a feature-fusion approach that combines GCN outputs with transformer encoder features.. The objective of these experiments was to determine whether the proposed adaptive masking strategy and the use of GCN as CPE provide advantages over simpler alternatives.

The proposed KDE-based adaptive masking strategy achieved the high-est average balanced accuracy (66.03%) across the 10 datasets, outperform-ing all alternative masking strategies on average. Although Channel-wise, Time-slot, and Partial Time-slot masking incorporate EEG-specific struc-tures, they were not inferior to the proposed KDE-based masking method but also achieved lower performance than random masking. Partial Time-slot Masking can increase the occurrence of heavily masked time slots and therefore satisfies the first objective described in Section 3.2, namely reducing the availability of strongly correlated channel information. 36 However, because the time slots are selected independently at random, it does not explicitly prevent simultaneous heavy masking of adjacent time slots. Consequently, it fails to satisfy the second objective described in Sec-tion 3.2, namely preserving temporal context by avoiding consecutive heavily masked time slots during reconstruction. These findings suggest that simply increasing the masking density of specific time slots is insufficient for effective representation learning. Additionally, preventing simultaneous heavy mask-ing of adjacent time slots is an important design component contributing to the effectiveness of the proposed KDE-based adaptive masking strategy. Figure 13 provides a visual comparison between the masking patterns generated by the proposed KDE-based adaptive masking strategy and Par-tial Time-slot Masking with within-slot masking proportions of 10–90%. As shown in Figure 13(a), the proposed method distributes heavily masked re-gions while avoiding long runs of consecutive heavily masked time slots. In contrast, Figure 13(b) shows several adjacent time slots with a high mask-ing density, highlighted by the red box. Because Partial Time-slot Masking selects time slots independently at random, heavily masked time slots can oc-cur consecutively without any mechanism to discourage such patterns. Thus, temporal dependencies across neighboring time slots may become unavailable for reconstruction. This example highlights a key difference between the two strategies: The proposed method preserves temporal context by avoiding consecutive heavily masked time slots, whereas Partial Time-slot Masking can unintentionally remove temporal information required for reconstruction. 

Comparison with Alternative GCN Architectures. To justify the use of GCN as a CPE mechanism, we compare the proposed architecture with two alternative GCN-based designs. The first variant, denoted as GCN-Only , removes the transformer encoder and performs both pretraining and downstream classification using only GCN features. The second variant, denoted as GCN-Feature , uses the same graph structure as the proposed method but incorporates the GCN output as an additional feature rather than as positional encoding. Specifically, the GCN features are added to the output of the transformer encoder before the classification head. In contrast, CABrain(PE) employs GCN outputs as CPEs by adding them to the patch embeddings before transformer processing. The results are presented in Table 11. Overall, CABrain(PE) achieved the highest average balanced accuracy (65.19%) among the three architectures, followed by GCN-Feature (63.46%) and GCN-Only (55.34%). The results in-37 dicate that graph convolution alone is insufficient to fully capture the complex temporal and spatial dependencies required for diverse EEG decoding tasks. Nevertheless, GCN-Only produced competitive results on several datasets. For example, its performance on Mumtaz2016 exceeded that of the proposed method, and its performance on SEED-V surpassed that reported for the early EEG foundation model BIOT. Furthermore, on FACED, PhysioNet-MI, ISRUC, and CHB-MIT, GCN-Only achieved higher balanced accura-cies than EEGNet. However, because EEGNet is a lightweight task-specific model trained without large-scale pretraining, these comparisons should be interpreted with caution.

Figure 14 presents the pretraining loss curves of CABrain and the previ-ous best-performing baseline CBraMod [29]. These findings suggest that, although 39 CBraMod achieves a marginally lower training loss, the proposed methods provide more transferable and generalizable representations. In particular, adaptive masking (CABrain(Mask)) appears to encourage the learning of more robust feature representations, resulting in improved downstream per-formance despite a slightly higher pretraining loss. The adaptive masking scheme reduces the contribution of strongly corre-lated channels and shifts the focus toward weaker or serial correlations. Con-sequently, the information available for reconstructing masked EEG patches becomes less reliable, which increases the pretraining loss. This trend is ob-served in Figure 14, with CABrain(Mask) attaining the highest final loss. Increased loss is also evident at the patch level. In Figure 15, the gray-scale intensity represents masked patches, with brighter tones indicating higher reconstruction loss. When adaptive masking was applied (right panel), a larger number of patches exhibited high losses, particularly in the regions highlighted by red circles. In these time slots, the masking ratio was high, and the lack of strongly or moderately correlated channel information re-sulted in increased reconstruction loss.

5. Conclusion 

This paper introduces CABrain, a correlation-aware EEG foundation model that integrates GCN–based CPE and an adaptive masking strategy for self-supervised pretraining. Extensive experiments on 10 diverse test datasets demonstrate that each approach individually achieves performance comparable or superior to that of state-of-the-art methods, while their com-bination often yields further improvements. Notably, both approaches are designed following an in-depth analysis of the key factors limiting the per-formance gains of existing methods. This highlights the systematic nature of our work and its contribution to overcoming fundamental challenges in EEG foundation model pretraining. Beyond addressing the limitations of existing methods, this study offers a novel perspective rather than incremental refinements. Specifically, this study represents the first attempt at incorporating graph-based representa-tions into CPE, which previously relied on 2D grid inputs. In addition, we propose an adaptive masking strategy for SSL, which has been extensively studied in the computer vision and natural language processing domains but, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously applied to EEG foun-dation models. Both proposed methods leverage the correlation information inherent in EEG signals, thereby reflecting a unified design principle. As correlation is not an absolute measure—unlike the number or arrangement of electrodes or the amplitude of signals—it is less sensitive to dataset vari-ability. A limitation of the proposed method is the difficulty of directly integrating the two proposed approaches. Because adaptive masking substantially alters the node features provided to the GCN-based CPE, we adopt a transfer-learning strategy instead of applying them simultaneously.
