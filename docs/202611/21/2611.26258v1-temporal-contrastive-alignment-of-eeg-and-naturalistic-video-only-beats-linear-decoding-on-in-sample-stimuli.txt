ABSTRACT 

Objective. Neural tracking, the temporal alignment between (features of) a continuous stimulus and the elicited brain re-sponse, enables decoding attention from electroencephalogra-phy (EEG). Heavily studied for speech and music, it remains largely unexplored for naturalistic video, relying on linear canonical correlation analysis (CCA). We investigate whether a nonlinear temporal contrastive model can strengthen EEG-video alignment, and examine the physiological and practi-cal basis of what it learns. Approach. We pair a lightweight EEG encoder with a projection of VideoMAE features, train-ing both branches via a correlation-based InfoNCE objective. The model is benchmarked against CCA under three general-ization regimes. To probe the basis and utility of the learned representations, we introduce a sham control with circularly shifted video features, a zero-shot transfer test to selective attention decoding on superimposed videos, and an evalua-tion against a group-averaged EEG reference. Main results. 

The model outperforms CCA on unseen subjects, matches CCA on held-out segments of seen videos, but falls behind on entirely novel videos. A sham control shows that this high accuracy on unseen subjects does not certify physically meaningful alignment: a model retrained on circularly shifted video features, with genuine temporal alignment destroyed, still reaches comparable accuracy on seen stimuli. Yet the coupling stays useful: on a zero-shot selective-attention de-coding task with seen stimuli, the model still beats CCA, since decoding needs only a strong statistical coupling, regardless of whether it is physiologically meaningful. Finally, against a group-averaged EEG reference, genuine, generalizable cou-pling is nonetheless learned, only masked in single-subject EEG by measurement noise and subject-specific response dif-ferences. 1. INTRODUCTION 

During the processing of continuous sensory stimuli such as natural speech or dynamic visual scenes, cortical neural ac-tivity synchronizes with the fluctuations of the input, creating a temporal alignment between the stimulus and the elicited electrophysiological response [1, 2]. This phenomenon, com-monly known as neural tracking, is modulated by attention, where attention acts as an amplifier leading to stronger tempo-ral coupling [3, 4, 5]. Because of this differential modulation, it is possible to decode the specific target of a person’s atten-tion and their overall level of attentional engagement directly from their brain activity. This activity is typically recorded using electroencephalography (EEG) due to its high temporal resolution and non-invasiveness. A key focus in recent years has been on translating proof-of-concept attention decoding into real-world applications, such as neuro-steered hearing aids and attention monitoring systems for clinical and edu-cational settings [2, 6]. However, such translation is non-trivial. Naturalistic stim-uli, such as speech, music, or video, are rich and highly com-plex, posing significant challenges for finding relevant fea-tures that robustly correlate with the EEG response [7, 8]. Moreover, to achieve true ecological validity, real-world ap-plications require single-trial decoding. In these scenarios, the model must extract reliable information from inherently noisy EEG signals without the luxury of averaging across multiple trials [6, 9]. Together, these constraints motivate investigating whether more expressive decoding models can form stronger temporal stimulus-EEG alignments and gener-alize well across diverse subjects and stimuli. Extensive research in the speech domain has historically focused on linear methods, primarily linear stimulus recon-struction (LSR) and canonical correlation analysis (CCA). arXiv:2611.26258v1 [eess.SP] 19 Nov 2026 LSR learns a linear mapping from EEG to the stimulus [7, 9, 10], whereas CCA finds linear projections of both modalities that are maximally correlated [11, 12]. Although data-efficient and interpretable, these models have limited capacity to capture nonlinear relationships between EEG and complex naturalistic stimuli. An early step was taken by de Taillez et al. [13], who extended the LSR framework with hid-den layers, nonlinear activations, and dropout, representing a minimal, meaningful, but ultimately non-replicable departure from purely linear models [6]. Ciccarelli et al. [14] bench-marked this nonlinear LSR variant alongside a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based model, showing that the CNN significantly outperforms both LSR and the nonlinear exten-sion, a finding that was also non-replicable [6]. Subsequent research explored a broader range of nonlinear architectures for EEG-stimulus alignment, including recurrent networks [15, 16], autoencoders [17, 18], and transformers [19, 20]. In parallel, stimulus representations evolved from hand-crafted features (e.g., speech envelopes, Mel spectrograms) [14, 15] to deep-learned semantic embeddings (e.g., Wav2Vec 2.0, HuBERT) [21, 22]. Orthogonal to the choice of feature, the way the two modalities are related has also changed: instead of regressing one modality onto the other, two-branch models map EEG and stimulus into a shared embedding space with a trainable encoder on each path, jointly optimized by a match-mismatch [23, 24] or, more recently, a contrastive [17, 20] objective, so that both representations are shaped by the alignment task itself. While these advances have improved decoding accuracy in certain conditions, their gains are frag-ile: higher-capacity models are more susceptible to exploiting dataset-specific shortcuts [25, 26, 27, 28], and performance often fails to generalize to new subjects, unseen stimuli, or different recording setups [6]. Whether these improve-ments reflect genuine advances in stimulus-EEG alignment, or merely better in-distribution fitting, therefore remains an open question. In the visual domain, the exploration of EEG alignment with naturalistic video stimuli remains limited. Early work by Dmochowski et al. [8] tested a variety of low-level video fea-tures and identified two (temporal contrast and optical flow) that could be correlated with EEG using CCA. However, Yao et al. [29] later demonstrated that these significant corre-lations were primarily driven by shot cuts (sudden changes in scene or camera angle). These cuts introduce abrupt dis-continuities that create artificial peaks in the extracted fea-tures while simultaneously eliciting strong visually evoked responses in the EEG. When tested on continuous, single-shot videos, these correlations became non-significant. To address this, the authors proposed object-based versions of the temporal contrast and optical flow features, successfully recovering significant correlations even in single-shot videos. To date, these remain the only features proven effective un-der such single-shot condition, and were subsequently uti-lized in a study applying CCA to decode attended videos in a superimposed-video paradigm [30]. It is worth noting that a parallel line of recent research focuses on classifying or re-constructing visual stimuli from EEG using deep generative models [31, 32, 33]. However, these approaches typically op-erate on static images or short, discrete video clips. They rely heavily on decoding overarching semantic information to per-form classification and reconstruction, which fundamentally differs from the continuous, stimulus-EEG temporal align-ment problem focused on in this paper. In this work, inspired by the advances in auditory EEG-stimulus alignment, we investigate for the first time whether introducing nonlinearity with neural networks and using more advanced deep video representations can improve temporal alignment between EEG and naturalistic video stimuli, com-pared to the CCA baseline. Aware of the pitfalls of data leakage and overfitting, the model is trained and evaluated under three generalization regimes: cross-subject (leave-one-subject-out), within-stimulus (leave-video-segment-out), and novel-stimulus (leave-one-video-out). For each regime, we dive into the underlying causes of success or failure, and iden-tify the key constraints that limit performance. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the datasets, the pre-trained video feature, the temporal contrastive model, and the evaluation protocol. Section 3 benchmarks the model against CCA under the three generalization regimes and on a down-stream attention-decoding task. Section 4 examines what the model actually learns and what limits it, and Section 5 con-cludes. 

2. METHODS 2.1. Data 

In this study, we worked with two publicly available contin-uous Video-EEG datasets collected at KU Leuven [34, 35]. Dataset I is our primary dataset for all model training and the three generalization regimes, while Dataset II provides a sepa-rate, held-out selective-attention test. These evaluation setups are detailed in Section 2.4.3 and Section 2.4.4, respectively. In both datasets, participants watched naturalistic videos un-der free-viewing conditions, with no fixation constraint, to preserve ecological validity. The corresponding EEG data were acquired using a 64-channel BioSemi ActiveTwo sys-tem at a sampling rate of 2048 Hz. To record eye move-ments, four electrooculogram (EOG) electrodes were placed around the participants’ eyes. The audio of all videos across both datasets was muted to prevent auditory-evoked neural responses from confounding the visual processing analysis. In both datasets, the EEG was preprocessed following the same procedure as the studies that released them [29, 30], comprising standard steps such as bad channel interpolation, rereferencing to the average of all channels, high-pass filter-ing with a cutoff frequency of 0.5 Hz, removing powerline noise at 50 Hz, and eye-artifact removal by regressing the EOG channels out of the EEG channels. For the present anal-ysis, the preprocessed EEG was downsampled to 30 Hz for the CCA baseline, matching the video frame rate [29, 30], and to 128 Hz for the contrastive model, which learns its own tem-poral filters and downsamples internally to the video-feature rate (Section 2.3.2). 

2.1.1. Dataset I: Video-EEG encoding-decoding dataset 

The first dataset [34] was designed to investigate the temporal correlations between natural, single-shot videos and EEG sig-nals during passive viewing. It includes two distinct subsets based on the visual complexity of the stimuli: • Single-Shot Subset: 19 participants passively viewed 13 continuous, single-shot videos (totaling approx-imately 63 minutes of data per participant). These videos depicted a single individual performing actions such as dancing, acrobatics, and mime. • Complex Scene Subset: A subset of 9 participants viewed an additional 24-minute continuous clip from a television episode. This stimulus introduced more complex visual dynamics, including shot cuts, multiple moving objects, and complex interactions. Since shot cuts introduce abrupt discontinuities that elicit strong event-related-potential-like responses in the EEG that may dominate the EEG-video coupling and overshadow the subtler neural tracking of the visual dynamics of interest [29], we focused our analysis on the single-shot subset (19 participants × 63 minutes of data). 

2.1.2. Dataset II: Selective Visual Attention Decoding Dataset 

The second dataset [35] was designed to evaluate EEG-based decoding of selective visual attention when participants were presented with superimposed video streams. This dataset also includes data from 19 participants (a separate cohort from Dataset I). The experimental paradigm used 14 single-shot videos (13 of which were inherited from Dataset I), combin-ing them into 7 superimposed video pairs. Each trial was structured to guide and test sustained selective attention: • Single-Object Phase (0-120s): Participants were pre-sented with a single video stream and instructed to pay attention to the person in the video. • Transition (120-122s): A two-second fade-in intro-duced a second, overlapping video stream. • Superimposed-Object Phase (122s-End, 165s on aver-age): Both videos were fully superimposed. Partici-pants were instructed to maintain their visual attention solely on the original target from the first phase, ac-tively ignoring the superimposed distractor. Each video pair was viewed twice with the target and distrac-tor roles reversed, leading to a total of 14 trials per partic-ipant. The dataset comprises 19 participants × 28 minutes of single-object viewing and 19 participants × 38 minutes of superimposed viewing. 

2.2. Video Feature Extraction 

Representation learning for video is an extensively studied topic in computer vision, with a wide variety of architectures proposed in the literature. For neural tracking, the key re-quirement is a representation that captures temporal dynam-ics rather than one optimized primarily for semantic classifi-cation. Previous work established that motion-based features (object-based optical flow) are effective for EEG-video align-ment [29, 30], motivating a step toward richer spatiotemporal representations that go beyond a rough ‘all-pixel’ average mo-tion feature. To this end, we chose VideoMAE [36], a masked autoencoder architecture that learns representations by recon-structing largely masked video patches. The masking applies both spatially and temporally, forcing the model to learn spa-tiotemporal features that capture the underlying dynamics of the video, making it a natural fit for temporal alignment tasks. All videos were first standardized to a common format: resampled to 16 Hz and resized to 224 × 224 pixels. To pre-serve the original aspect ratio, frames were rescaled so that the longer side fit within 224 pixels and then symmetrically zero-padded to a square 224 × 224 canvas. These preprocess-ing choices match VideoMAE’s original training configura-tion, which uses a 16-frame vision transformer base and an input resolution of 224 × 224 . Each preprocessed video was partitioned into consecutive, non-overlapping 1-second win-dows of 16 frames. The model was loaded with a publicly released checkpoint on Hugging Face (MCG-NJU/videomae-base). Each input video segment yields 8 temporal token po-sitions (16 frames / tubelet size 2) and 14 × 14 = 196 spa-tial patches per temporal position, for a total of 1568 tokens, each represented by a 768-dimensional embedding. The full hierarchy of representations of a 1-second video window in-cludes the patch-embedding output together with the outputs of all 12 transformer blocks, giving 13 layers of hidden states: 

(13 , 8, 196 , 768) . The high dimensionality of this representa-tion remains a challenge for downstream EEG alignment, so we applied a pooling step to reduce the dimensionality while preserving the temporal axis. 

2.2.1. Attention-weighted spatial pooling 

We pooled spatially, layer by layer: for each of the 13 layers, we collapsed its 196 spatial patches into a single feature vec-tor per temporal position, leaving the layer axis untouched. Rather than uniform spatial averaging, we used an attention-weighted pooling scheme driven by the model’s own atten-tion, so that spatially salient regions contribute more to the resulting representation. For a given layer, the multi-head at-tention tensor was averaged over heads, and for each token we computed the mean attention it receives across all query positions. These per-token saliencies (8 temporal × 196 spa-tial) were normalized with a softmax over the spatial dimen-sion, which concentrates the pooling weights on the most at-tended regions. Figure 1 shows the attention maps of an ex-ample video for all 12 layers of the first window, next to the corresponding video frame, where the temporal axis is col-lapsed by averaging over the 8 positions (for display only). The attention maps reveal that early layers concentrate on the background and actively suppress the performer. This pattern inverts around layer 4, where the model begins to favor the performer and high-contrast structures, such as the light re-flections on the floor. We observed that this progression is consistent across different time windows and distinct videos, though the onset of this inversion occasionally shifts one layer later. Within each layer, the hidden states were then reduced to a weighted sum over the 196 spatial patches using these weights, yielding one 768-dimensional vector per temporal position and per layer. The patch-embedding layer, which has no associated attention map, was pooled using the at-tention weights of the first transformer block as the nearest available proxy. After pooling, each 1-second window was represented by a feature tensor of shape (13 , 8, 768) , that is, layers × temporal positions × features. Concatenating the features across a T -second video, we obtained a feature ten-sor of shape (T, 13 , 8, 768) .

2.2.2. Temporal differencing 

Before using these features for EEG alignment, we asked whether they primarily reflect the video’s temporal dynam-ics or a static, position-dependent component. We inspected the pooled features of each video via principal component analysis (PCA): for each layer, we flattened the temporal axis to a single dimension of size 8T (for a single video) and projected the 768 -dimensional features onto their first two principal components. The resulting scatter plots re-vealed that the features of each video collapsed into eight tight clusters, consistent with the eight temporal positions within each 1-second window. Figure 2a (left) shows one such case. To further characterize how feature similarity depends on temporal separation, we computed a normalized autocorrelation function from the 8T × 8T pairwise cosine-similarity matrix comparing each token of a video with every other token. The normalized autocorrelation function is the mean similarity as a function of temporal lag ∆, obtained by averaging the entries on the ∆-th off-diagonal of the simi-larity matrix. The function (Figure 2b, blue) shows a strong periodic pattern with period exactly one second: tokens at the same temporal position across different 1-second windows are highly similar. Moreover, this peak similarity shows only a marginal decrease as temporal distance increases. Both the clustering and the periodic autocorrelation were present in every transformer layer and in every video. This pattern is consistent with the influence of VideoMAE’s positional embeddings: each window is encoded independently, but the token at a given temporal position carries the same fixed po-sitional component in every window. This shared positional component, likely compounded by static background and se-mantic features, dominates the representation. Consequently, it drives a strong similarity between temporally aligned to-kens, obscuring the genuine temporal variation we aim to study. To remove this, we applied a first-order temporal differ-ence across windows at each fixed temporal position, replac-ing each window’s features by their difference from the pre-ceding window:

2.3. Temporal Contrastive Model 

To go beyond linear CCA while remaining tractable on a small dataset, we adopted a simple two-branch architecture trained with a contrastive objective. One branch processed 

Tw-second EEG windows; the other processed Tw-second video feature windows. Following CCA’s use of temporal correlation as the similarity measure, both branches produced one scalar per temporal position, and similarity was computed as the Pearson correlation between the two output sequences. The overall architecture is illustrated in Figure 3.

2.4. Training and Evaluation 

2.4.1. Batch construction and window augmentation 

We sampled Tw-second windows from the training set to form a batch of B matched EEG-video pairs. We did not assign a fixed start position for each video, as this would create a dis-crete set of (video, window-index) identities. A model could then memorize a unique code for each such unit without learn-ing any genuine temporal structure, a form of table lookup that would fail on unseen stimuli. Instead, the B windows were drawn sequentially on-the-fly, with a running record of already-chosen start times per video. A candidate start t0 was valid for a given video only if |t0 − t∗

Start position was drawn uniformly within the available non-conflicting intervals of the chosen video. At each step the video was sampled propor-tional to the number of its remaining valid integer start posi-tions, so videos with no room left dropped out automatically. To encourage the model to learn representations that generalize across timescales, we applied a window aug-mentation strategy. For each batch, we randomly sam-pled a window length Tw from a uniform distribution over 

{5, 10 , 15 , 20 , 25 , 30 } seconds. For the chosen (video, start, 

Tw), a subject was drawn uniformly from the training pool and the EEG window was sliced and z-scored per channel. The video features were sliced from the pre-extracted tensor at the same integer second boundaries. We will investigate three regimes, each with their own train, test, and validation split (see Section 2.4.3). In valida-tion, we used fixed Tw = 30 -second windows extracted with a 5-second stride, to avoid the randomness of start positions affecting the evaluation. In test, we used the same 5-second stride but additionally evaluated the model on multiple win-dow lengths to assess its robustness to timescale variation. 

2.4.2. Training 

Parameters were optimized using Adam, with learning rate set under a cosine-annealing schedule. The optimization hy-perparameters were tuned once per evaluation regime and re-served validation set (Section 2.4.3), not per test fold, using Optuna, choosing the best trial with the lowest validation loss out of 50 trials with epoch-level median pruning [38]. The search space is summarized in Table 1. Each trial was trained for up to 75 epochs of 60 batches each, with early stopping on the validation loss (patience of 10 epochs). 

2.4.3. Evaluation under three generalization regimes 

We separated three different generalization questions: 1. Leave-one-subject-out (LOSO): Let S be the subject set ( |S| = Ns). Subject s0 is reserved as the valida-tion set. Each of the remaining Ns − 1 subjects is held out in turn as the test subject si, while the other Ns − 2

subjects are used for training. All videos appear in both splits. This scheme tests cross-subject generalization. 2. Leave-video-segment-out (LVSO): Partition each video into Nf = 8 contiguous, non-overlapping segments. Fold f0 of each video is reserved as the validation set, while each of the remaining Nf − 1 folds is held out in turn as the test fold fi. All subjects and all videos appear in both splits. This regime tests within-manifold generalization, i.e., to unseen parts of seen videos. 3. Leave-one-video-out (LOVO): Let V be the video set (|V| = Nv ). Video v0 is reserved as the validation set. Each of the remaining Nv − 1 videos is held out in turn as the test video vi, while the other Nv − 2 videos are used for training. All subjects appear in both splits. This regime tests off-manifold generalization, i.e., to unseen videos. The choice of the reserved subject s0, fold f0, and video v0

affects the hyperparameter tuning and early stopping. There-fore, per regime we repeated the training and evaluation with three different reserved choices, which we will refer to as ‘hy-perparameter configurations’. Results for each test subject, fold, or video were averaged over the three hyperparameter configurations in which it was tested. All three regimes oper-ate exclusively on Dataset I. To evaluate the model, we used pairwise match-mismatch (MM) accuracy, a standard metric in neural tracking [12]. Given a video window and two EEG windows, one of which is the true match and the other an imposter, the model needs to identify which EEG window corresponds to the video. A dual formulation is to ask, given an EEG window and two video windows, which video is the true match. The model gives a similarity score for each pair, as defined in Equation 8. 2.5. CCA baseline 

We compared the contrastive model against a CCA baseline from previous studies [29, 30]. In those studies, object-based optical-flow features were extracted from 30-Hz videos, the EEG was downsampled to 30 Hz, both modalities were han-kelized to incorporate temporal information (15 lags spanning 

−467 to 0 ms for the video feature and 3 lags spanning −33 to 33 ms for each EEG channel), and were correlated using CCA. The sum of the first two canonical correlation coeffi-cients served as the similarity score for the MM task. We followed the same procedure and parameter settings, with a single deviation: those studies fit a separate CCA per sub-ject, whereas we fit one CCA on the EEG and video features of the subjects in the training split, concatenated along the time axis. To keep the results directly comparable, the CCA baseline was trained and tested under exactly the same three regimes as the contrastive model (Section 2.4.3); the reserved validation set was excluded from fitting but served no further purpose, as CCA requires no hyperparameter tuning or early stopping. It was scored with the same pairwise accuracy met-ric, batch samplers, and seeds.

4. DISCUSSION 4.1. High Accuracy Under LOSO Does Not Guarantee Physically Meaningful EEG-Video Alignment 

In Section 3.1 we showed that the contrastive model out-performs CCA under LOSO without using video identity as a shortcut, and the window augmentation of Section 2.4.1 further discourages the model from memorizing a discrete code for each (video, window-index) pair. To probe whether a more subtle form of shortcut exists, we constructed a sham dataset in which the genuine temporal alignment between EEG and video is destroyed while all other structure is pre-served: we applied a per-video deterministic circular shift (≥ 60 s), the same one for all subjects, to the video features and left the EEG unchanged. The model was retrained on this sham dataset and evaluated under LOSO, reusing the hyper-parameter configuration selected on real data with Subject 1 as the reserved validation subject. Architecture, learning rate, batch size, and schedule were therefore identical across the two conditions, which differ only in whether genuine EEG-video alignment is present in the training data. Surprisingly, the sham model reaches accuracy close to that of the cor-responding real-data Subject 1 run (Figure 9a), whereas the CCA baseline, given the same sham data, collapses to chance. The contrastive model can therefore reach high LOSO accu-racy without genuine temporal alignment in the training pairs, through a route that is unavailable to CCA. 10 20 30

To see how this is possible, it helps to view the train-ing set as a whole rather than as a collection of windows. Even though circular shift breaks the temporal alignment be-tween EEG and video, the model may eventually build a com-plex, physically arbitrary mapping from the video-feature se-quences to the corresponding EEG traces over the entire train-ing set (i.e., across all subjects), which is then applied to each window. This is what window augmentation cannot resolve, because it only prevents the model from memorizing a fixed set of window slots, not from overfitting or memorizing a function that maps video content to EEG content. This func-tion is unlikely to extrapolate to unseen videos, but it can in-terpolate to windows within the training videos, and, through the subject-general EEG response component, to EEGs of unseen subjects viewing the same videos. This mirrors the pattern across the three regimes: any stimulus-specific corre-spondence, whether genuine but confined to the seen video content or synthesized through a physically arbitrary map-ping, is available wherever the model only needs to interpo-late between different window sizes and window start-times within seen videos, inflating LOSO (Section 3.1) and match-ing CCA under LVSO (Section 3.2), and it vanishes precisely where the model must extrapolate to novel videos (LOVO, Section 3.3). The sham experiment exemplifies a synthesized corre-spondence that can inflate LOSO accuracy without physically meaningful EEG-video coupling, but it does not necessar-ily imply that the model trained on real data also relies on such a nonsensical correspondence. Deep networks have the capacity to fit pure noise [39]; yet when genuine struc-ture is present, they prioritize learning simple, generalizable patterns before resorting to memorization [40, 41]. Here the physically meaningful coupling is plausibly simpler than the synthesized one. We therefore expect the model trained on real data to exploit the physically meaningful coupling whenever it is learnable. The learning dynamics are consistent with this hypothesis. Figure 9b plots mean training and validation accuracy across LOSO folds against epoch. The training curves are nearly superimposed: both conditions plateau around 90% (reaching 

85% at 17 versus 19 epochs) 4. The validation curves diverge: the real model reaches 70% in 8 versus 19 epochs for the sham model, and 75% in 15 versus 40 epochs, and its checkpoint is selected 10 epochs earlier on average. During training, the goal is to reduce the loss as much as possible, and the model will exploit whatever structure is available. Since it does not necessarily require genuine video-to-EEG coupling, the training curves are remarkably similar when training on real versus sham data. When transferring to an unseen subject, only the found patterns of the subject-independent, stimulus-locked EEG component carries over, which is what the validation curves track. This divergence suggests that the real model may exploit the simpler, physi-cally meaningful coupling early in training, whereas the sham model must rely on a more complex, artificial transformation of the sham videos to these general EEG patterns, which takes longer to develop [40, 41].

In summary, the sham experiment shows that high accu-racy under LOSO does not by itself certify that the model has learned physically meaningful EEG-video alignment: a reproducible but synthesized correspondence fit to the seen stimuli is sufficient to explain it. At the same time, the faster emergence of cross-subject transfer in the real model sug-gests that genuine coupling is present and is exploited when available, which will be further demonstrated in Section 4.3 with actual held-out video data. Part of what is learned may nonetheless be stimulus-specific, either genuine but confined to particular video content, or synthesized and built up in the later stages of training. 

4.2. Physically Meaningful Coupling Is Not Always Re-quired: Attention Decoding as Template Matching 

High LOSO accuracy may (at least partially) rest on a syn-thesized correspondence rather than (or in addition to) physi-cally meaningful video-to-EEG coupling. What does this im-ply for the practical utility of the model? For applications that require generalization to unseen stimuli, the synthesized correspondence is insufficient, as shown in the LOVO setting. But many applications do not require generalization to unseen stimuli, only to unseen subjects. For example, a lecture video is fixed and known at training time, and the model is used to monitor attention of new students watching the same video. In this case, the model can operate as a template matcher: it learns a reproducible template for each video and matches in-coming EEG against these templates. Whether the templates encode physically meaningful coupling and/or a synthesized correspondence to the EEG responses is irrelevant, as long as they are reproducible across subjects. In the downstream attention decoding task of Section 2.4.4, every evaluated video was seen during training, so the task is close to the LOSO regime, and each video serves equally often as the attended and as the unattended stream, so neither competing template is better learned than the other. Attention amplifies EEG responses to the attended stream, and therefore leads to a stronger match with the corresponding video tem-plate, regardless of whether the template reflects physically meaningful coupling or a synthesized correspondence. In other words, this is possible because the discriminating infor-mation lies on the EEG side rather than in the fidelity of the video mapping. Therefore, the advantage of the contrastive model over CCA on this task demonstrates practical utility and attention modulation, but, consistent with Section 4.1, it does not by itself certify that the model has learned how to extract features from any video that are predictive for the EEG response. 

4.3. Generalizable Video-EEG Coupling Is Learned but Masked by Individual Noise 

The LVSO tie and the LOVO reversal between CCA and the contrastive learning model raise a question: does the con-trastive model fail to learn generalizable EEG-video coupling, or does it learn a generalizable coupling that is simply masked by individual noise in single-subject EEG? To separate these, we constructed a cleaner, group-averaged EEG reference, which suppresses individual noise because averaging cancels what is not shared across subjects. The regimes themselves are untouched: the model is fitted exactly as under LVSO or LOVO, so the held-out video segment or video is unseen during training for every subject, and the group-averaged reference enters only at evaluation time. When evaluating subject n on a held-out segment or video, the group-averaged reference is the average of the EEG embeddings of the other 

Ns − 1 subjects computed on that same held-out segment or video, time-aligned with the trial being scored. With this reference, we defined two tasks analogous to the standard video-EEG MM task: the video-avgEEG task matches the video embedding against the group-averaged ref-erence EEG embedding, and the EEG-avgEEG task matches subject n’s individual EEG embedding against the group-averaged reference (with subject n removed). Because individual noise is largely averaged out of the reference, video-avgEEG estimates how well the learned alignment generalizes once individual noise is removed, and comparing it against EEG-avgEEG isolates whether the limitation lies on the video side or the individual-EEG side. The video-avgEEG accuracy is much higher than the stan-dard (single-subject) video-EEG accuracy in all cases (Fig-ure 10). Even under LOVO, where the model underperforms CCA on individual EEG, video-avgEEG reaches a mean ac-curacy around 75% on unseen videos. Genuine, generaliz-able EEG-video coupling that transfers to unseen videos is therefore learned even in the regime where the model appears to fail; it is simply masked by individual noise in the EEG embedding. Moreover, video-avgEEG on held-out videos or video segments is higher than EEG-avgEEG: the video em-bedding matches the group EEG better than an individual sub-ject’s EEG matches it. Both comparisons point to the same conclusion, that individual noise, rather than an absence of learned alignment, is what limits the contrastive model’s de-codability under LVSO and LOVO. This does not conflict with the strong LOSO performance of Section 3.1. Individual noise limits every regime, LOSO included. What differs across regimes is not the noise, which is identical since the same subjects and EEG are used through-out, but the strength of the signal relative to that noise. For seen videos (LOSO, and partly LVSO), the model can addi-tionally exploit stimulus-specific correspondence, whether genuine but confined to the seen content or synthesized (Section 4.1). For unseen videos (LOVO), the model can only leverage generalizable video features (omitting video-specific features), which is weaker in single-subject EEG. 4.4. Group-Level Alignment Is Easy; Single-Subject De-coding Is Limited by Signal-to-Noise Ratio 

Prior efforts to strengthen EEG-video alignment have con-centrated on finding better video representations, from hand-crafted features [8, 29] to the pretrained VideoMAE features used here, in the hope of explaining more of the EEG vari-ance. The group-averaged reference allows us to examine this direction separately for the shared and the individual part of the EEG. For the shared part, the video-avgEEG accu-racy already reaches around 90% in both LVSO and LOVO with the simple hand-crafted optical-flow feature of the lin-ear CCA baseline, even higher than the contrastive model’s video-avgEEG (around 75% under LOVO; Section 4.3). On 30-second windows, the MM task against the group-averaged reference thus already approaches its ceiling, leaving little room to observe further gains from better video representa-tions on this task. The coupling with the individual EEG, which is the quan-tity that matters at deployment, is considerably weaker. The gap between the video-EEG and video-avgEEG tasks reflects a low single-subject signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) (Section 4.3): the stimulus-locked signal is weak relative to individual noise in a single subject. This bottleneck can be addressed from two sides. On the signal side, a more expressive video represen-tation could capture a stronger stimulus-locked response and thereby raise the single-subject SNR. On the noise side, meth-ods that suppress individual noise in the EEG could achieve the same, a direction that has so far received far less attention than feature design. 

4.5. Implications for Evaluation, Modeling, and Data 

The contrastive model is positioned differently against the CCA baseline under the three regimes (Sections 3.1–3.3), and the sham experiment (Section 4.1) shows that a high LOSO accuracy can be deceptive, reflecting a synthesized correspon-dence rather than physically meaningful coupling. Any sin-gle regime therefore gives an incomplete, and potentially mis-leading, picture. This leads to what we regard as the most im-pactful implication of this work: models for neural tracking of natural stimuli should be evaluated under the strictest regime, holding out subjects and stimuli jointly (leave-subject-and-video-out), whenever the dataset permits it. This recommendation is not specific to video, as stud-ied here, but applies equally to neural tracking of speech. In that domain, increasingly complex nonlinear models share the same capacity to synthesize a correspondence instead of cap-turing genuine neural coupling. Such models are commonly evaluated with only one axis held out, subjects or stimuli, but rarely with both held out simultaneously [14, 23, 24, 25, 42], which limits the confidence that can be placed in their re-ported gains. Our findings clearly demonstrate the risks of us-ing a regime that holds out only one axis and leaves the other exploitable, despite the prevailing assumption that these are rigorous evaluation schemes that are not prone to shortcuts. When a dataset does not permit joint subject-and-stimulus holdout (e.g., due to small scale, as in our case), LOSO and LOVO should at least be reported together, since the gap be-tween them exposes how much of the apparent performance depends on having seen the stimulus or the subjects during training. To improve generalization under LVSO and LOVO, we evaluated several interventions: constraining output dimen-sionality via low-rank temporal bases, implementing a multi-component structure, applying a multi-scale correlation loss, augmenting EEG channels, and introducing an auxiliary EEG-denoising loss. None yielded meaningful improve-ments over the base model. This outcome suggests two non-mutually exclusive interpretations: either the architec-ture requires a fundamental redesign, or the dataset inherently limits the current model’s capabilities in size and/or diversity. Both scenarios point to the same underlying requirement. If the dataset is the bottleneck, scaling it is directly necessary; conversely, if a more expressive architecture is required, it will demand more data to prevent overfitting. Ultimately, fu-ture progress is gated by the size and diversity of the available data. This turns attention to how such data should be collected. The fact that the model ties CCA under LVSO but underper-forms under LOVO is unsurprising: the latter demands ex-trapolation to unseen videos, whose distribution differs from the training stimuli, and datasets in this area reuse a small set of videos, leaving a deep model little basis to generalize to novel stimuli. Broadening the stimulus set is thus likely more valuable than recording more subjects on the same videos. 

5. CONCLUSION 

We present the first study of temporal contrastive alignment between EEG and naturalistic video. Our approach aligns EEG signals with pretrained VideoMAE features using a lightweight two-branch encoder trained via a correlation-based InfoNCE objective. We benchmarked this model against a CCA baseline across three generalization regimes and a downstream selective visual attention task. Results show that the contrastive model outperforms CCA on seen stimuli, both in cross-subject (LOSO) settings and down-stream attention decoding. However, it performs only on par with CCA within the stimulus manifold (LVSO) and falls behind when forced to extrapolate to entirely novel videos (LOVO). Two diagnostic analyses explain this pattern. A sham experiment shows that high accuracy on seen stimuli does not certify physically meaningful EEG-video alignment: asynthesized, physically arbitrary correspondence, fit over the training stimuli, is enough to reproduce it. Yet genuine, gen-eralizable coupling is also learned. It reaches good accu-racy against a group-averaged EEG reference even on unseen videos, and is simply masked in single-subject EEG by in-dividual noise, i.e., measurement noise and individual differ-ences in the response to the video. Building the coupling with this clean reference is already achieved by the linear CCA baseline with a simple motion feature, which points to the SNR of single-subject EEG as the primary bottleneck. These findings carry two practical messages. For ap-plications with a fixed, known stimulus set, genuine cross-stimulus coupling is not required, and template matching with stimulus-specific features over seen stimuli already suffices. The demand for genuine, generalizable alignment becomes binding only for open-set applications that must handle novel stimuli. For the field more broadly: neural tracking of natural stimuli, whether video or speech, should be evaluated with subjects and stimuli held out simultaneously, since single-axis regimes such as LOSO or LOVO, despite their rigorous repu-tation, can substantially overstate genuine alignment. Further progress is gated by the size and diversity of the data. Ideally, datasets should be broadened to include more stimuli, and methods should raise the single-trial SNR, both by capturing more stimulus-locked signal and by suppressing individual noise, the latter having received far less attention so far. 6.
