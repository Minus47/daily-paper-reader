ABSTRACT 

Stereo-electroencephalography (SEEG) is an invasive tech-nique used in the presurgical evaluation of drug-resistant epilepsy to support the localization of the Epileptogenic Zone (EZ). SEEG data are particularly complex to analyze, as they include hundreds of components, named channels , each associated with a different brain site, with interactions vary-ing in both time and frequency . In this study, we propose a new framework for SEEG analysis, based on the Canonical Polyadic (CP) decomposition, to extract the latent modes underlying seizure generation. We model SEEG signals as a third-order tensor (channel × time × frequency), and factorize the tensor into rank-1 factors. We then exploit the channel-specific factor as an unsupervised feature set for identify-ing the EZ. The framework is validated on SEEG-recorded seizures from 35 patients with drug-resistant epilepsy. The results show that the joint combination of multiple frequency bands significantly enhances classification performance. 

Index Terms — Stereo-electroencephalography, epilepsy surgery, tensor decomposition, functional connectivity 

1. INTRODUCTION 

> This paper has been submitted to the IEEE International Symposium on Biomedical Imaging (ISBI) 2027.

The goal of epilepsy surgery is to resect the Epilepto-genic Zone (EZ), defined as the cortical area indispensable for the generation of seizures [1]. When non-invasive evalu-ation is inconclusive, EZ location can be inferred via Stereo-Electroencephalography (SEEG), an invasive procedure to record electrical activity from cortical and subcortical struc-tures through intracranial electrodes with multiple contacts. The result is a multi-dimensional signal, in which each chan-nel reflects the activity of a brain site. In clinical practice, SEEG recordings are reviewed by highly specialized neuro-physiologists, with particular attention to the transition from the inter-ictal (between seizures) to the ictal period (during seizures) [2]. This analysis, based on visual inspection, al-lows one to identify the Seizure Onset Zone (SOZ), the region where seizures originate, and a key proxy for the EZ. In recent years, many techniques have been developed to support SEEG interpretation, analyzing the spectral energy of single channels or functional connectivity between them. The second option is particularly relevant given that seizures can originate from the interaction of distributed brain structures [3]. In particular, SEEG signals can be modeled as time-varying graphs, where nodes represent channels and edges represent connectivity relationships. A variety of techniques have been proposed to estimate SEEG connectivity, with re-sults that change depending on the frequency band analyzed. The SOZ channels tend to desynchronize within the first sec-onds of seizure, considering slow oscillations [4], and in-crease high-frequency connectivity as ictal discharges prop-agate through the brain [5]. Hence, integrating connectivity information across different frequency bands is fundamental to provide a comprehensive description of SOZ. To cope with the inherent complexity of SEEG data, we can apply dimensionality reduction techniques to discover latent patterns behind brain activity. In this context, [6] pro-posed a joint matrix factorization to identify connectivity subgraphs shared across different seizures. More recently, [7] applied Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) to sepa-rate frequency-specific modes involved in seizure generation. However, these approaches do not integrate information from different oscillations: in [6] connectivity is computed in a single frequency band, whereas in [7] frequency is only used to sort the decomposition modes. To jointly capture the ef-fect of different frequency components, SEEG signals can be represented as tensors whose modes are nodes (or links), time, and frequency [8, 9]. This strategy was explored in [10], which builds wavelet-power tensors of inter-ictal SEEG activity, and in [11], which considers connectivity tensors during seizures. Nevertheless, these studies were validated in very small cohorts, and none of them assessed whether tensor modeling can support the identification of the EZ. Inspired by the previous works in the literature, we de-velop a framework where the features of SEEG signals are modeled as a channel × time × frequency tensor. We then ap-ply the Canonical Polyadic / Parallel Factors (CP/PARAFAC) arXiv:2611.26137v1 [eess.SP] 19 Nov 2026 decomposition to factorize the tensor into a reduced number of components. The factors associated with the spatial di-mension are then exploited as an unsupervised feature set to identify the SOZ channels. We validate the framework on ictal data from 35 patients with drug-resistant epilepsy, who underwent SEEG monitoring at La Timone Hospital (Mar-seille, France). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study applying tensor decomposition to ictal SEEG data from a large patient cohort. Our results show that the integration of multiple frequencies into a single tensor model improves classification performance, suggesting that our framework is a promising tool for analysis of SEEG signals.

3. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS 3.1. Dataset and Settings 

We retrospectively analyzed SEEG data of 35 patients from the EPINOV database of La Timone Hospital, (Marseille, France). The patients were selected according to the follow-ing inclusion criterion: at least one seizure starting with low-voltage fast activity. The signals were sampled at fs = 500 

Hz and then converted to a bipolar montage. For each patient, we selected an epoch of T = 50 s spanning 35 s s before and 

15 s after the electrographic seizure onset, i.e., the start of fast oscillations. We segment each epoch into windows lasting 

τ = s , with a shift of ∆τ = 0 .25 s .Following the model in Sec. 2.1, we computed the energy Eqc [w], entropy Hqc [w], and connectivity Iqc,h [w]

for each channel c, link (c, h ), window w, and frequency band q ∈ Q = {δ, θ, α, β, γ low , γ high }, where δ = [2 , 4) ,θ = [4 , 8) , α = [8 , 14) , β = [14 , 30) , γ low = [30 , 70) ,γhigh = [70 , 150) Hz. To capture ictal-related changes for each feature, we applied a Z-score normalization using the first 30 s (i.e., the first 120 windows) of each recording to estimate the mean ( E) and standard deviation ( STD ) of each feature. For energy, this yields

Following the model in Sec. 2.2, we then constructed the tensors X E , X H , and X I from the remaining 20 s of each epoch, with the seizure starting after 5 s. The tensor dimen-sions are determined by the number C of channels, which is patient-specific, the number W of windows, and the number 

Q of frequency bands. In our experiments, we vary the por-tion of the ictal signal analyzed and the composition of the frequency band set Q, obtaining different results for W ∈

[1 , W max ] and Q ∈ { 1, . . . , 6}, where Wmax = 77 denotes the last window of the epoch. Finally, we set the rank of the de-composition to R = 6 , and the percentiles used to compute the weighted degrees to H = {75 , 80 , 85 , 90 , 95 }.We used the unsupervised feature vectors dE (c), dH (c),and dI (c) as inputs to a logistic regression classifier to iden-tify channels labeled as part of the SOZ. Ground truth was defined by the clinical annotations provided by neurophysi-ologists following visual inspection of the SEEG recordings. Classification performance was assessed using the Area Un-der the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) and the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC). Our classification task presents strongly imbalanced classes and, thus, the AUPRC is a preferable metric for system evaluation[16]. Since the goal of this study is to assess the intrinsic discriminative power of the unsupervised features, performance was evaluated in a within-patient setting, while cross-patient generalization is left for future work. 3.2. Numerical Results 

We first consider the edge scenario where the feature set Q

includes a single frequency band, so that the tensor has shape 

C × W × 1 for energy and entropy, and C2 × W × 1 for MI. Fig. 2 reports the AUPRC, averaged across the patient cohort, as a function of the feature used to build the tensor and of the extension of the signal analyzed. We note that per-formance exhibits different trends depending on the feature used to build the tensor. The energy-based model achieves its best results during the first seconds of the seizure (which starts after 5 s), particularly when using the α and the γhigh 

bands. Instead, the entropy-based model performs best in the low portion of the frequency spectrum and at later stages of the seizure. Finally, the MI-based model yields the highest AUPRC overall, with maxima in the δ and θ bands and ap-proximately 5 s after seizure onset. Fig. 3 reports the distribution across patients of the time-averaged AUROC and AUPRC as a function of the frequency bands included in the tensor. Each distribution is shown as a box plot, where the central line is the median, the edges are the first and third quartiles, and the whiskers are the 5-th and 95 -th percentiles. We denote by Q = 2 the configura-tion with the δ and θ bands and, for increasing Q, the con-figurations obtained by adding one band at a time. For the energy-based tensor, the best performance is obtained with 

γhigh , while including additional bands does not improve ei-ther AUROC or AUPRC. The entropy-based tensor shows the opposite trend: the best performance is obtained with the low-frequency bands, and it decreases monotonically as higher portions of the spectrum are included. For the MI-based tensor, the performance trends are sim-ilar to those of entropy, even though with higher AUROC and AUPRC values in all frequency configurations. In this case, combining multiple bands yields more robust unsuper-vised features: all the multi-band configurations improve the AUROC and AUPRC in both the upper tails with respect to the single-band models. In particular, the configuration inte-grating all frequencies ( Q = 6 ) reaches mean AUROC and AUPRC values of 0.887 and 0.655 , respectively. Performing a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test [17], with the alterna-tive hypothesis that Q = 6 achieves a higher AUPRC than single-band tensors, we obtain significant differences in all comparisons. The best single-band configuration ( θ) is out-performed with a p-value p = 0 .001 , while all remaining configurations are outperformed with p < 0.001 .

4. CONCLUSIONS 

In this work, we proposed a new framework to model SEEG signals across space (channels), time, and frequency. We built three SEEG tensor models that characterize the signal through its energy, entropy, and functional connectivity. We applied the CP/PARAFAC approach to decompose the tensor into rank-1 factors, and exploited the spatial factor as an un-supervised feature set for EZ definition. The framework was evaluated on a dataset of 35 patients who underwent SEEG monitoring at La Timone Hospital (Marseille, France). Our results show that the performance trends across fre-quency bands depend on the feature used to build the ten-sor, and that integrating multiple frequency bands in the same model improves the identification of the EZ in terms of AU-ROC and AUPRC. Future work will address the validation of the framework on an independent patient cohort, using cross-validation strategies to assess the generalizability of the re-sults beyond the within-patient settings.
