ABSTRACT
EEG-to-motion decoding remains challenging because noisy scalp EEG is dif-
ficult to map directly to continuous kinematics. We present NeuroMotion, a
framework that aligns EEG representations to discrete motion primitives for con-
tinuous motion decoding across heterogeneous paradigms, including full-body
free movement, lower-limb treadmill locomotion, and upper-limb freewill reach-
ing and grasping. A VQ-based motion prior discretizes continuous motion into
reusable primitives that serve as both alignment prototypes and intermediate pre-
diction targets. Prototypical and supervised contrastive objectives align EEG
embeddings to motion-derived prototypes and organize them according to prim-
itive assignments. An E2M Transformer predicts motion-code sequences from
the learned EEG representations, while a final geometric refinement stage im-
proves continuous trajectory reconstruction through motion-level training. Under
a last-session test protocol, NeuroMotion achieves the best mean reconstruction
performance among the compared EEG decoding baselines across all three datasets,
with the clearest gains on full-body motion in both reconstruction accuracy and
distribution-level quality. Multi-subject ablations further support the contributions
of EEG-to-primitive alignment, token prediction, and refinement. Code is available
at https://github.com/Liuyi2351433/NeuroAction.

INTRODUCTION
Recent years have seen significant advances in brain-computer interfaces (BCIs), enabling applications
ranging from communication restoration and neurorehabilitation to closed-loop neuromodulation
and human-computer interaction. Among these applications, a central goal is to translate neural
activity into actionable motor commands for external effectors, such as robotic arms, prosthetic limbs,
exoskeletons, and humanoid agents. Achieving this goal requires robust EEG-to-motion decoding:
inferring continuous human movement from non-invasive brain signals.
Despite steady progress, EEG-to-motion decoding remains highly challenging. Most existing methods
are developed for specific motor paradigms, such as hand movement, reaching, or simplified gait
cycles, and typically regress low-level kinematic variables directly from EEG (Bradberry et al.,
2010). While effective in constrained laboratory settings, such direct regression becomes difficult to
scale across heterogeneous motor behaviors, long temporal horizons, and cross-session variations.
A key reason is that EEG-to-motion decoding is not a strict one-to-one mapping. Scalp EEG
provides noisy and spatially coarse measurements of population-level neural dynamics, rather than
precise muscle-level commands. Neurophysiological studies further suggest that motor control is
organized hierarchically, relying on internal forward models and modular motor primitives instead
of explicitly specifying all joint trajectories (Wolpert et al., 1998; Mussa-Ivaldi, 1999). Optimal
feedback control theory also suggests that neural control prioritizes task-relevant variables rather than
exact reproduction of detailed kinematics (Todorov & Jordan, 2002). These observations motivate
using motion-derived primitives as structured targets for EEG representation learning and as an
intermediate representation for continuous motion reconstruction.

Meanwhile, EEG-motion study is moving from controlled single-task settings toward naturalistic
and heterogeneous behaviors, including treadmill walking with closed-loop BCI control (He et al.,
2018), freewill reaching and grasping (Thapa et al., 2025), and full-body locomotion across complex
terrains (Brantley et al., 2018). The shift requires that EEG-to-motion frameworks go beyond
task-specific regressors and can be applied across upper-limb, lower-limb, and full-body motor
paradigms.
Recent progress in motion generation and vision-language-action modeling suggests that continuous
motion can be discretized into reusable tokens. Vector-quantized autoencoders have been widely used
to tokenize high-dimensional human motion for Transformer-based generation (van den Oord et al.,
2017; Guo et al., 2022b; Zhang et al., 2023), and VQ-VLA further shows that quantization is also
useful for low-dimensional action trajectories. However, EEG-to-motion decoding requires a different
use of motion quantization: EEG is noisy, subject-dependent, and not an explicit symbolic command,
but it is temporally aligned with motion. We therefore use motion codes both as structured motion-
primitive prototypes for EEG representation and as discrete intermediate targets for reconstruction.
To this end, we propose NeuroMotion, a framework that aligns EEG representations to discrete
motion primitives for continuous motion decoding. NeuroMotion learns dataset-specific primitives
with a VQ tokenizer, aligns EEG embeddings to their codebook representations through prototypical
and supervised contrastive learning, predicts motion-code sequences with an E2M Transformer, and
refines decoded trajectories through continuous motion-level supervision. We evaluate NeuroMotion
on three EEG-motion datasets covering full-body movement, treadmill locomotion, and freewill
reaching and grasping. Under a strict last-session test protocol, NeuroMotion achieves the best mean
reconstruction performance among the compared EEG decoding baselines across all three datasets,
with the clearest gains on full-body motion. Multi-subject ablations further support the contributions
of EEG-to-primitive alignment, token prediction, and geometric refinement.
Our key contributions are summarized as follows:
• We adopt EEG-to-primitive alignment, using motion-derived codebook prototypes and primitive
assignments to structure continuous EEG representations via prototypical and contrastive learning.
• We develop NeuroMotion, a hierarchical EEG-to-motion framework that integrates motion tokeniza-
tion, representation alignment, and motion-level refinement for continuous motion reconstruction.
• Across three heterogeneous EEG-motion datasets, NeuroMotion achieves the best mean recon-
struction performance over baselines under a strict last-session test protocol, with the clearest gains
on full-body motion and multi-subject ablations supporting alignment and staged decoding.

2.6
DIFFERENTIABLE MOTION-LEVEL REFINEMENT
After EEG-to-motion token generation, NeuroMotion performs a final geometric refinement stage
to improve continuous trajectory accuracy. Token-level supervision encourages correct primitive
prediction, but it does not directly optimize the decoded motion sequence. We therefore introduce
a differentiable decoding path from token logits to continuous motion: given token logits P ∈
RB×N×K, we use straight-through Gumbel-Softmax to obtain differentiable hard token selections:

continuous motion trajectories.
2.7
TRAINING PROCEDURE
NeuroMotion is trained in three stages. In Stage 1, we train the motion VQ-VAE on motion sequences
only, producing a dataset-specific motion codebook and decoder that define the discrete motion-prior
space. Each paired motion sequence is then quantized into a token sequence using nearest-neighbor
codebook assignment.
In Stage 2, the motion tokenizer is frozen, and the EEG encoder and E2M Transformer are trained to
predict motion tokens from EEG. The training objective combines token prediction with codebook-
based PCL and supervised contrastive alignment:
Lstage2 = Ltok + λpclLpcl + λsupconLsupcon.
(10)
In Stage 3, we initialize from the Stage 2 model and apply the geometric refinement objective in
Sec. 2.6. The VQ encoder and codebook remain fixed, while the EEG encoder, E2M Transformer, and
VQ decoder are updated using continuous motion-level supervision. Overall, training proceeds from
learning discrete motion primitives to EEG-to-primitive alignment and token prediction, followed by
continuous motion-level refinement.
2.8
INFERENCE
At inference time, only EEG is required. Given an EEG segment, the EEG encoder first extracts
neural embeddings, and the E2M Transformer predicts a discrete motion-token sequence. Hard
argmax token IDs are mapped to their frozen codebook embeddings and decoded by the VQ decoder
into the final continuous motion trajectory. Geometric refinement is a training stage, so inference
requires no iterative motion optimization.

EXPERIMENTS
3.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS
Datasets. We evaluate NeuroMotion on three public EEG-motion datasets spanning heterogeneous
motor paradigms: full-body multi-terrain locomotion (FBM) (Brantley et al., 2018), treadmill lower-
limb locomotion (TM) (He et al., 2018), and freewill reaching and grasping (FR) (Thapa et al., 2025).
For FBM, we use subjects s02–s10, excluding s01 due to synchronization issues, and 59 EEG
channels after removing FCz; for TM, we evaluate six subjects using all 60 channels, excluding S03
and S04 due to data quality issues; and for FR, we use all 31 channels from s05, s07, s13, s15,
and s22, the five subjects with complete three-session recordings. Dataset acquisition schedules and
the distinction between cross-day and session-disjoint evaluation are detailed in Appendix B.1.
Motion representation. We use dataset-specific motion targets according to the available kinematic
signals. For FBM, we first select 22 joints from the original 23-joint representation by removing the
L3 joint and reorder them to match the SMPL-style HumanML3D joint convention; the resulting
motion is then converted to the 263-D HumanML3D-style representation. For TM, the target is a 6-D
lower-limb joint-angle sequence measured from bilateral hip, knee, and ankle goniometers. For FR,
the target is the synchronized three-axis wrist acceleration sequence.
Data split. We use a strict session-wise split to avoid temporal leakage. For each subject, the last
available session is used for testing, while earlier sessions are used for training. The motion VQ-VAE

Training details. Across datasets, kinematic signals are low-pass filtered at 5 Hz, downsampled to
20 Hz, and temporally aligned with EEG, while EEG is band-pass filtered at 0.1–48 Hz, re-referenced
using common average reference (CAR) (Delorme & Makeig, 2004), and downsampled to 100 Hz.
Paired signals are segmented into 2 s windows with a 0.5 s stride. TM and FR motion targets are
min-max normalized to [0, 1], Channel-wise z-score normalization is applied to EEG as the first
operation of the EEG encoder. The default VQ codebook sizes are 32/16/12 and the motion-token
counts are 10/10/5 for FBM/TM/FR, respectively. Dataset-specific model widths, optimizer settings,
learning-rate schedules, iteration budgets, and training costs are provided in Appendix B.2.
3.2
COMPARISON WITH ADAPTED EEG-TO-MOTION BASELINES
Baselines. Since EEG-to-motion decoding has been studied under different motor paradigms and
output formats, we compare NeuroMotion with representative baselines including the upper-limb
decoder MTRT (Wang et al., 2023), the gait-specific NeuroDyGait (DY) (Fu et al., 2025), and
general-purpose EEG models EEGConformer (EC) (Song et al., 2023), STTransformer (ST) (Song
et al., 2021), DeepConvNet (DCN) (Schirrmeister et al., 2017), and ContraWR (Yang et al., 2023).
We additionally compare linear regression (LR), ridge regression (RR), and an unscented Kalman
filter (UKF) on all three datasets (Nakagome et al., 2020; Luu et al., 2016). All methods use the same
dataset splits, preprocessing, and prediction targets; detailed baseline adaptation and training controls
are provided in Appendix B.4. Table 1 reports cohort-level mean±std performance.
Overall performance and uncertainty. Figures 4 and 3 complement the aggregate metrics with
representative qualitative reconstructions across the three datasets. NeuroMotion captures the domi-
nant temporal structure of full-body motion on FBM and follows the major joint-angle and wrist-
acceleration trends on TM and FR, while fine-scale deviations remain visible.
Table 1 shows that NeuroMotion Stage 3 attains the highest mean reconstruction performance among
the compared methods on all three datasets. On FBM, PCC improves from RR’s 0.219 ± 0.158 to
0.522 ± 0.238; R2/RMSE improve from NeuroDyGait’s −0.212 ± 0.203/0.897 ± 0.251 to 0.110 ±
0.354/0.718 ± 0.229, respectively. Positive mean R2 implies non-trivial variance capture, although
absolute reconstruction accuracy remains limited and between-subject variability is substantial.
The gains on TM and FR are smaller and accompanied by substantial between-subject variability,
especially given the limited cohort sizes. Complete subject-level results, improved-subject counts,
and paired statistical comparisons are provided in Appendix D.7. Overall, the performance gains are
clearest on FBM, while conclusions on TM and FR remain more limited.

4
DISCUSSION
NeuroMotion uses discrete motion primitives as an intermediate representation between noisy EEG
and continuous kinematics. Its main contribution is aligning continuous EEG embeddings to motion-
derived prototypes and integrating this alignment with token prediction and motion-level refinement.
A common formulation supports FBM, TM, and FR, using a dataset-specific motion tokenizer and
subject-specific EEG-to-motion models.
Multi-subject ablations support the contributions of alignment and staged learning, while EEG
input controls demonstrate sensitivity to ordered temporal dynamics. Frequency-band analysis
(Appendix D.2) and representation analyses provide complementary diagnostics. Because the motion
codebook is learned solely from kinematics, the resulting tokens should be interpreted as reusable
motion primitives with task-specific EEG correspondence rather than independently established
physiological motor states.
Limitations. The study is limited by small subject cohorts and offline evaluation. The low mean
R2 on FBM and negative values for some subjects indicate limited absolute reconstruction accuracy
and consistency across subjects. Although TM and FR include cross-day testing, longer-term EEG
nonstationarity remains unresolved, while FBM lacks verified day-level metadata. The discrete
VQ motion prior may introduce quantization error and suppress fine-grained kinematic variation.
Reported inference timings characterize model-side computation only and do not establish end-to-end
closed-loop performance. Larger population studies, longer-term evaluation, and online validation
remain important future directions.
5
CONCLUSION
We presented NeuroMotion, a framework that aligns EEG representations to discrete motion primitives
and integrates motion-token prediction with geometric refinement for continuous motion decoding.
Across three dataset-specific instantiations, the method improves mean reconstruction metrics over
the compared baselines, with the strongest statistical evidence on FBM end-effector reconstruction.
Multi-subject ablations, temporal controls, and representation analyses support structured EEG–
motion alignment. Cross-day testing and offline profiling characterize current capabilities, while
broader population studies and online closed-loop evaluation remain necessary.

APPENDIX
A
RELATED WORK
A.1
EEG-BASED MOTION DECODING
Decoding movement-related information from non-invasive EEG has long been studied in BCI re-
search. Early studies showed that low-dimensional kinematic variables, such as movement direction,
hand velocity, or joint trajectories, can be decoded from EEG in controlled paradigms (Bradberry
et al., 2010; Robinson & Vinod, 2016). Deep learning further enabled end-to-end spatiotemporal
modeling of minimally processed EEG using CNN, RNN, and compact convolutional architec-
tures (Schirrmeister et al., 2017; Lawhern et al., 2018), while recent Transformer-based models
improve long-range temporal modeling for EEG decoding (Song et al., 2023).
EEG-based motor decoding has been studied across a range of movement paradigms. Upper-limb
decoding commonly focuses on hand movement, reaching, or low-dimensional arm trajectories (Brad-
berry et al., 2010; Robinson & Vinod, 2016), with sequence models such as MTRT reconstructing
continuous arm motion using Transformer-based temporal modeling (Wang et al., 2023). Lower-limb
and gait decoding methods study cyclic locomotion dynamics, motion artifacts, and nonstationarity,
using CNN/LSTM architectures (Tortora et al., 2020), hierarchical or graph-based designs (Fu et al.,
2026), and phase-aware representation learning for cross-session or cross-subject robustness (Fu et al.,
2025). Recent work has also explored structured interfaces for whole-body or humanoid-oriented
control, such as E2H, which uses an EEG-to-keyword and keyword-to-motion pipeline (Duan et al.,
2024). Together, these studies provide important foundations for modeling neural activity associated
with upper-limb, lower-limb, gait, and whole-body movement.
For the gait comparison, NeuroDyGait serves as the later task-specific reference. Our comparison
covers direct regression and learned continuous intermediate representations; it does not establish
superiority over every possible adapted generative model.
A.2
MOTION AND ACTION TOKENIZATION
Discrete representations have become an effective tool for modeling continuous motion and action
sequences. In text-to-motion generation, vector-quantized autoencoders tokenize high-dimensional
human motion into compact codebooks (van den Oord et al., 2017), enabling Transformer-based
autoregressive generation conditioned on textual or semantic inputs (Guo et al., 2022b; Zhang et al.,
2023). These methods show that learned motion codebooks can absorb low-level kinematic details
and provide a structured latent space for long-horizon motion synthesis. EEG2MOTION (Peng et al.,
2026) extends this text-to-motion paradigm to motion synthesis from EEG recorded during action
observation. It combines a pretrained HumanML3D VQ-based motion prior with global EEG–motion
contrastive alignment and EEG-conditioned masked-token generation.
Motion and action tokenization has also been explored in vision-language-action and robotic con-
trol models. VQ-style action quantization, as in VQ-VLA (Wang et al., 2025), discretizes low-
dimensional control trajectories into reusable action tokens. This suggests that quantization can serve
as a general mechanism for representing both high-dimensional human motion and lower-dimensional
robotic action sequences.
A.3
REPRESENTATION ALIGNMENT FOR NEURAL DECODING
Robust neural decoding often benefits from learning structured latent representations rather than
relying solely on direct output supervision. Recent EEG foundation models discretize EEG into neural
tokens and train sequence models with language-model-like objectives, enabling unified inference
across multiple EEG tasks (Jiang et al., 2025). Related studies show that aligning latent neural
dynamics across sessions can improve decoding stability and robustness (Karpowicz et al., 2025). In
brain-to-image reconstruction, approaches such as MindEye demonstrate that contrastive alignment
between brain activity and semantic embedding spaces can learn informative neural representations
for retrieval and reconstruction (Scotti et al., 2024). Similar alignment strategies have also been
explored in EEG-to-text decoding to bridge neural activity and language representations (Wang et al.,
2024).

NeuroMotion learns discrete primitives from motion and aligns continuous EEG representations
to their codebook vectors. These primitives provide both representation-level supervision and
intermediate prediction targets for continuous motion reconstruction. EEG tokenizers used for
pretraining or classification therefore address a different intermediate representation.Likewise, generic
motion generation and EEG foundation encoders require an EEG-conditioning or synchronized
kinematic decoding interface before they can be evaluated on this task. Testing such adaptations is
useful future work; their absence does not imply that they are ineffective.
B
EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS
B.1
DATASET ACQUISITION AND SESSION PROTOCOLS
FBM records EEG and full-body kinematics during continuous overground multi-terrain locomotion,
including level walking, ramps, and stairs (Brantley et al., 2018). We use subjects s02–s10,
excluding s01 due to synchronization issues. For each subject, the last available session is held
out for testing. The dataset does not provide explicit visit/day metadata sufficient to establish a
cross-day split, so we interpret this protocol as session-disjoint cross-trial evaluation rather than
verified cross-day generalization.
TM records 60-channel EEG and bilateral lower-limb joint angles during treadmill walking and
closed-loop BCI control (He et al., 2018). We evaluate six subjects, excluding S03 and S04 due to
data quality issues. Sessions 01 and 02 were recorded during the first visit, while Session 03 was
recorded during a second visit within seven days. Training on Session 01/02 and testing on Session
03 therefore provides a cross-day evaluation.
FR records 31-channel EEG and three-axis wrist acceleration during voluntary reaching-and-
grasping (Thapa et al., 2025). We evaluate s05, s07, s13, s15, and s22, the five subjects
with complete three-session recordings. Sessions 1–3 were recorded on different days; training on
the earlier sessions and testing on Session 3 therefore constitutes cross-day evaluation.
Accordingly, the main TM and FR protocols evaluate session-disjoint cross-day generalization,
whereas FBM provides session-disjoint evaluation without verified day separation. None of these
protocols establishes stability over multi-week or multi-month intervals.
B.2
MODEL AND TRAINING DETAILS
Signal preprocessing.
For all datasets, kinematic signals are low-pass filtered at 5 Hz, downsampled
to 20 Hz, and temporally aligned with EEG. FBM motion is converted into the 263-D HumanML3D-
style representation described in Sec. B.3, while TM and FR motion targets are min-max normalized
to [0, 1]. EEG signals are band-pass filtered at 0.1–48 Hz, re-referenced using common average
reference (CAR) (Delorme & Makeig, 2004), and downsampled to 100 Hz.Channel-wise z-score
normalization is then applied as the first operation of the EEG encoder rather than as an offline
preprocessing step.
Windowing and token length.
All datasets are processed into temporally aligned EEG-motion
windows of length 2 s with a stride of 0.5 s. Kinematic signals are downsampled to 20 Hz, so each
motion window contains 40 frames. The motion tokenizer temporally downsamples each window
into a sequence of motion tokens. The default token counts are Ne = Nm = 10 for FBM/TM and
Ne = Nm = 5 for FR, corresponding to nominal durations of 0.2 s and 0.4 s per token. The model
predicts one motion-token sequence per EEG window.
Architecture hyperparameters.
For FBM, we use a VQ codebook size of 32. The EEG encoder
hidden dimension and E2M hidden dimension are both set to 512. The EEG encoder contains 6
Transformer layers, and the E2M Transformer contains 16 layers with 8 attention heads.
For TM and FR, which contain lower-dimensional kinematic targets, we use smaller codebooks and
model widths. Specifically, TM uses a VQ codebook size of 16, while FR uses a VQ codebook size
of 12. For both datasets, the EEG encoder and E2M hidden dimensions are set to 256. The EEG
encoder contains 6 Transformer layers, and the E2M Transformer contains 16 layers with 8 attention
heads.

Stage 1: VQ motion prior training.
We train a separate motion tokenizer for each dataset using
only the kinematic training data. Test sessions used in EEG-to-motion evaluation are excluded from
VQ training. The VQ tokenizer is trained with AdamW using learning rate 2 × 10−4, batch size 1024,
weight decay 1 × 10−3, 100k iterations, 1k warmup iterations, and a cosine learning-rate schedule.
The reconstruction loss weight is set to 1.0, and the commitment loss weight is set to 0.02. After
training, the VQ encoder, codebook, and decoder define the dataset-specific discrete motion prior.
Stage 2: EEG-to-motion token learning.
With the motion tokenizer frozen, we train the EEG
encoder, neuro-motion alignment module, and E2M Transformer to predict motion-token sequences
from EEG. The training objective is
Lstage2 = Ltok + λpclLpcl + λsupconLsupcon.
(11)
We set λpcl = 1.0 and λsupcon = 1.0, and use temperature τ = 0.07 for both contrastive objectives.
Stage 2 is trained with AdamW using learning rate 1 × 10−3, batch size 512, weight decay 1 × 10−3,
12k iterations, 1k warmup iterations, and a cosine learning-rate schedule.
Stage 3: geometric refinement.
Starting from the Stage 2 checkpoint, we perform geometric
refinement by optimizing the decoded continuous motion with an MSE loss. During this stage, the
EEG encoder, E2M Transformer, and VQ decoder are updated, while the VQ encoder and codebook
remain fixed. We use AdamW with learning rate 1 × 10−5, batch size 64, weight decay 1 × 10−3, 2k
iterations, 1k warmup iterations, and a cosine learning-rate schedule.
For FBM, Stage-3 MSE supervises only the 30-D end-effector subset in Appendix C; TM/FR
supervise all 6/3 target dimensions. The FBM 263-D supervision variant in Appendix D.1.4 changes
only this supervision scope.
Training cost.
All experiments are trained on a single NVIDIA L4 GPU. Stage 1 is run once per
dataset and takes approximately 6 h for FBM and 2 h for TM/FR. Stages 2–3 are subject-specific.
With subject-dependent convergence and early stopping, Stage 2 runs for 2,000–12,000 iterations
(10–60 min), and Stage 3 for 500–2,000 iterations (3–12 min), within the nominal budgets above.
Subject-specific training therefore takes 13–72 min. Original and additional experiments share the
same preprocessing, split, normalization, and training settings except for explicitly controlled factors.

B.4
BASELINE ADAPTATION AND TRAINING
For a controlled comparison, all baselines use the same EEG preprocessing, session-wise train/test
splits, dataset-specific target representations, normalization, and evaluation metrics as NeuroMotion.
Each baseline retains its original backbone, while only the dataset-specific input interface and
regression output head are adapted where necessary.
On FBM, all methods predict the same 263-D HumanML3D-style representation over 40 motion
frames. For task-specific EEG-to-motion baselines, namely NeuroDyGait and MTRT, we retain their
continuous sequence-decoding structures, adapting the input and output channel dimensions and
making moderate adjustments to internal network depth. For the general-purpose EEG baselines
(DeepConvNet, EEGConformer, STTransformer, and ContraWR), we use a common MLP readout on

FBM with widths 256 →2048 →10,520, followed by reshaping to 40 × 263. On FR and TM, these
general-purpose baselines instead use a single linear projection from their final feature representations
to the complete motion window, followed by reshaping to 40 × 3 and 40 × 6, respectively. The
corresponding targets are the normalized 3-D wrist-acceleration sequence on FR and 6-D lower-limb
joint-angle sequence on TM.
For each deep-learning baseline, dropout is selected from {0.0, 0.1, . . . , 0.9}. Training uses learning
rate 10−4, cosine scheduling, a 200-epoch budget, and early-stopping patience 10. These controls
standardize the prediction task and baseline optimization protocol; backbone capacities and the staged
NeuroMotion training procedure are not forced to be identical across methods.
The added LR, RR, and UKF baselines are adapted from the public EEG-neural-decoding implemen-
tation (Nakagome et al., 2020). LR/RR flatten the complete 2-s EEG window and jointly predict all
40 motion frames; RR uses α = 1. UKF preserves frame-wise recursive state estimation and the
nonlinear observation formulation of Li et al. (2009). Reformulating UKF as joint whole-window
regression would substantially change its state and observation models and recursive inference proce-
dure, so we retain the reference decoding formulation. UKF uses a first-order state model, four EEG
taps at 20 Hz, a one-frame EEG lag, and transition/observation ridge coefficients of 1 and 5. Each
window starts from the training-state mean. EEG and target standardization are fitted on the training
split. All three methods use the same dataset-specific targets, session splits, and evaluation metrics as
the other methods.
Regarding EEG2GAIT, NeuroDyGait directly compares against it, reports stronger gait-decoding
performance, and shares the same first author; we therefore use NeuroDyGait as the later and
stronger gait-specific reference. Moreover, the EEG2GAIT release lacks components required for
full reproduction without undocumented assumptions.
EEG2MOTION (Peng et al., 2026) is not included as a directly adapted baseline because its pretrained
motion generator is coupled to the HumanML3D motion representation and codebook. It cannot be
directly reused for TM joint angles or FR wrist accelerations, which require task-specific motion
tokenizers, decoders, and corresponding generator adaptation. FBM also requires adaptation to the
recorded skeleton and motion domain. In addition, EEG2MOTION synthesizes observed actions
from action-observation EEG and emphasizes semantic matching and generative quality, evaluated
using R-Precision, MM-Dist, FID, and Diversity. Our task reconstructs synchronized trajectories
from EEG recorded during actual movement, with PCC, R2, and RMSE as the primary metrics.

EVALUATION METRICS
We evaluate EEG-to-motion decoding with RMSE, PCC, and R2 on all three datasets. For FBM,
these reconstruction metrics are computed on the 30-D end-effector position subset of the 263-D
HumanML3D-style motion vector, corresponding to the XYZ coordinates of 10 limb joints: LKnee,
RKnee, LHeel, RHeel, LFoot, RFoot, LElbow, RElbow, LWrist, and RWrist. The 0-based indices are
DFBM = [13:19, 22:28, 31:37, 55:67],
where each range is a zero-based, half-open slice, giving 30 dimensions in total. For TM, the
metrics are computed on the 6-D bilateral lower-limb joint-angle sequence from hip, knee, and ankle
goniometers. For FR, the metrics are computed on the 3-D wrist-acceleration sequence. For FBM,
we additionally report FID and Diversity in a pretrained motion-embedding space. We compute
each metric per subject and then average across subjects; reported standard deviations describe
between-subject variability. Paired comparisons use two-sided paired t-tests across subjects, with
uncorrected p-values reported in Appendix D.7.

Effect of VQ codebook size. Table 4 expands capacity analysis to three subjects per dataset: FBM
S02/S07/S10, TM S02/S06/S07, and FR S05/S07/S22. Under the same end-to-end evaluation, the

models generally remain competitive, although the ordering is not monotonic across all intermediate
sizes. The performance reduction is most visible on FBM, while TM and FR exhibit smaller changes.

D.2
FREQUENCY-BAND ABLATION STUDY
We conduct a frequency-band ablation to examine which EEG spectral components are most infor-
mative for motion decoding under our experimental setting. For each subject and session, we apply
band-pass filtering to the EEG signals and train/evaluate NeuroMotion using the same preprocessing,
architecture, data split, and optimization protocol. We compare individual frequency bands, including
delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma, together with an all-band setting using the full preprocessed
EEG spectrum. Reconstruction performance is reported using PCC and RMSE, where higher PCC
and lower RMSE indicate better decoding.
Figure 7 summarizes the FBM results. Low-frequency components provide the strongest predictive
information under this setting. Delta-band and all-band EEG show higher mean PCC and lower
mean RMSE than the higher-frequency bands, whereas alpha, beta, and gamma filtering generally
degrades reconstruction performance. These results suggest that NeuroMotion relies more strongly
on low-frequency EEG structure for naturalistic locomotion decoding, although the present analysis
does not isolate neural activity from other low-frequency signal components.
This trend is consistent with prior EEG-based gait decoding studies. For example, Luu et al. (2016)
showed that lower-limb joint angles during treadmill walking can be decoded from delta-band / slow-
cortical-potential EEG using an Unscented Kalman filter. More broadly, low-frequency EEG has
been associated with gait-cycle-related cortical dynamics and long-timescale sensorimotor integration
during rhythmic movement (Gwin et al., 2011; Knaepen et al., 2015). Higher-frequency components
in mobile EEG can also be more affected by muscle activity and motion-related artifacts, which may
reduce their reliability when used in isolation (Delorme & Makeig, 2004).

D.8
INFERENCE COST AND TIMING SCOPE
We profile the default models offline on a single NVIDIA L4 GPU using batch-1 FP32 PyTorch
eager execution, with 100 warm-up runs and five repeats of 1,000 measurements. Tables 16 and 17
report the number of parameters, serialized checkpoint size, MACs per input window, peak allocated
GPU memory, mean/P95 latency, and throughput.The full-model parameter counts in Tables 1 and 5
include the frozen motion encoder, whereas the inference-model counts in Table 16 exclude it. Model
latency measures GPU forward execution only. Post-window latency starts from a preprocessed EEG
window in CPU memory and ends with the reconstructed motion returned to CPU memory, including
host–device transfers.
Across the three default models, the largest post-window P95 latency is 21.610 ms on FBM, with
peak allocated GPU memory of 325.4 MiB. These measurements characterize only the model-side
computation after a preprocessed 2-s EEG window becomes available; they exclude EEG acquisition,
preprocessing, feedback, and control. No complete online closed-loop BCI experiment has been
conducted. End-to-end acquisition-to-action latency, feedback stability, and online calibration
therefore remain to be evaluated.

Input/Output. The EEG encoder maps a raw EEG window X ∈RB×C×T to a sequence of

F
FURTHER DISCUSSION
F.1
LIMITATIONS.
First, our experiments are restricted to a small number of subjects, reflecting the current scarcity
of synchronized EEG–motion datasets and limiting population-level generalization (Brantley et al.,
2018; Paulo et al., 2025). Second, our motion interface relies on a VQ-VAE tokenizer: discretizing
continuous human motion into a finite codebook provides a strong inductive bias and stabilizes long-
horizon generation, but it also introduces quantization error and restricts expressivity to the learned
vocabulary, potentially attenuating fine-grained kinematics and stylistic nuances. Encouragingly,
recent text-to-motion models have begun to reflect on this limitation and explore alternatives that inter-
face autoregressive backbones with continuous motion latents (e.g., VAE latents) and use lightweight
diffusion modules for high-fidelity synthesis, mitigating the symbolic–continuous mismatch induced
by tokenization (Zhu et al., 2026). Adapting such continuous-latent or diffusion-bridged motion priors
to EEG-conditioned generation is a promising direction, but requires revisiting token-level alignment
objectives and efficient online inference. Finally, TM/FR cross-day evaluation and offline L4 profiling
address limited aspects of generalization and efficiency. FBM has no verified day metadata, long-term
drift is untested, and a bidirectional encoder requires the available observation window. Streaming
acquisition, feedback stability, and online user adaptation remain open challenges.
F.2
BROADER IMPLICATIONS.
Broadly, this work suggests that successful neural decoding of complex behavior may require
representational alignment rather than direct signal-to-trajectory mapping (Karpowicz et al., 2025).
Our framework offers a general blueprint for linking neural data to structured generative models,
potentially extending beyond motion to other high-dimensional behavioral or perceptual domains.
