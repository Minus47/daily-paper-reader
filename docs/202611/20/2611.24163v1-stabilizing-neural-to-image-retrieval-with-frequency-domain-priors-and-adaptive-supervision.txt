# Stabilizing Neural-to-Image Retrieval with Frequency-Domain Priors and Adaptive Supervision 

YaoYang Liu, ZhuoShi Tang, Haopeng Lai, Weijun Sun*, Xiaozhao Fang*, Jie Wen, Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract —Visual neural decoding aims to infer perceived visual stimuli from brain activity, offering important insights into human visual cognition while enabling transformative applica-tions in brain computer interfaces and artificial intelligence. Recent advances in pretrained vision-language models have provided a semantically rich representation space for this task. However, reliable alignment between EEG/MEG signals and visual embeddings remains challenging because neural responses are noisy, highly variable across trials, and subject dependent. To address this issue, we propose a unified neural-to-image retrieval framework that improves retrieval performance by aligning neural and image representations in a shared se-mantic space. The framework integrates dual-stream adapta-tion, frequency-domain calibration, and phase-guided tempera-ture modulation to promote more robust cross-modal semantic alignment. Specifically, dual-stream adaptation models a low-dimensional shared subspace with correlation regularization to suppress redundant noise and enhance cross-modal consistency. Frequency-domain calibration conditionally modulates neural representations using amplitude and phase statistics. Phase-guided temperature modulation further adjusts the supervision strength in contrastive learning according to the stability of neural responses. Experiments on THINGS-EEG and THINGS-MEG under both intra-subject and inter-subject settings demon-strate that the proposed framework consistently outperforms strong baselines. These results suggest that robust neural-to-image retrieval under heterogeneous neural conditions benefits from a stable shared semantic space, frequency-informed rep-resentation calibration, and adaptive supervision. Code will be released at github.com/wanfkuabc/Geometry-Regularized-Brain-Embeddings. 

Index Terms —Cross-Modal Retrieval, Brain-Computer Inter-face, Contrastive Learning, Low-Dimensional Adaptation, Visual Neural Decoding, CLIP.

Among available recording modalities, EEG and MEG are particularly attractive because they provide millisecond-level temporal resolution, relatively low acquisition cost, and flexible experimental deployment. These advantages make them well suited for studying the fast dynamics of visual cognition and for building scalable neural decoding systems that go beyond small closed set recognition settings [5], [6], [7], [8]. At the same time, the fine temporal structure captured by EEG and MEG suggests that they may contain rich information about evolving perceptual representations, provided that this information can be translated into a semantic space suitable for retrieval. Recent progress in large-scale vision-language models, es-pecially CLIP, has created a promising foundation for retrieval-based neural decoding [2], [4], [9], [10], [11], [12]. Instead of formulating neural decoding as conventional closed set classification, CLIP-based neural-to-image retrieval maps neu-ral responses into a pretrained visual embedding space and performs matching in a shared semantic space. This paradigm is attractive for several reasons. First, the pretrained visual space carries category-level and semantic structure learned from massive image-text supervision, which is difficult to obtain from neural data alone. Second, retrieval in such a space naturally supports zero-shot evaluation and cross-category generalization, which are much closer to the open-vocabulary setting of real-world perception [4], [9]. Third, using a frozen visual backbone allows the model to inherit a stable semantic reference while concentrating learning capacity on the neural side. However, the success of this paradigm ultimately depends not only on the quality of the pretrained visual space, but also on whether noisy neural observations can be mapped into that space in a sufficiently stable and transferable manner. This alignment step remains the main bottleneck of cur-rent neural-to-image retrieval systems. Most existing methods follow a standard recipe that combines a pretrained visual encoder, a neural encoder, and a contrastive objective in a shared embedding space [4], [10], [13], [12]. Although effective, this formulation often treats alignment as a largely uniform projection problem and applies similar supervision across samples, batches, and subjects. For EEG and MEG, such a static treatment is often too coarse [4], [10], [12], [13], [14], [15]. Neural responses are not only noisy, but also highly variable across trials and exhibit substantial distribution shifts across subjects. As a result, the learned neural embeddings can fluctuate substantially in feature scale, dimension usage, and geometric organization across mini-batches, which in turn makes similarity estimation less reliable. Under these condi-tions, simply increasing encoder complexity does not directly arXiv:2611.24163v1 [cs.CV] 18 Nov 2026 2

solve the core problem, because the difficulty lies less in raw representation capacity than in the stability of the alignment pathway itself. What is needed is a mechanism that conditions neural representations before and during contrastive learning so that they can be organized more consistently with pretrained visual embeddings, while remaining robust to heterogeneous neural response quality. Motivated by this view, we propose a unified framework for neural-based neural-to-image retrieval. Our method keeps the CLIP visual semantic space frozen and improves retrieval by conditioning the neural pathway at several complementary levels. Specifically, we introduce dual-stream adaptation and batch-wise stabilization to regularize the geometry of neural embeddings before cross-modal matching. We further incor-porate frequency-domain priors by extracting amplitude and phase statistics from neural signals and using them to calibrate the learned representation through conditional modulation. In addition, we derive a phase-based control signal to adapt the contrastive temperature during training, allowing the super-vision strength to respond to differences in neural response stability without requiring an explicit difficulty estimator [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]. Related Work This section reviews prior research most relevant to our problem from four perspectives: neural visual decoding and re-trieval, shared brain-visual semantic spaces, parameter-efficient adaptation and alignment stabilization, and frequency-domain modeling with adaptive supervision. Rather than providing a purely taxonomic survey, we emphasize how these lines of work motivate the need for a retrieval framework that remains stable under noisy and heterogeneous EEG/MEG conditions. 

A. Visual Decoding and Retrieval from Neural Signals 

Decoding visual content from brain activity has long been a central topic in brain-computer interfaces and computational neuroscience [1], [3]. Early studies mainly focused on fMRI because of its high spatial resolution, but its low temporal resolution and limited experimental flexibility constrain its ability to characterize fast visual dynamics. By contrast, EEG and MEG provide millisecond-level temporal measurements and are therefore more suitable for studying the temporal evolution of visual perception [22], [23]. On this basis, a broad range of models, including convolutional architectures [24], [25], [26], temporal modeling networks, and Transformer-based designs, have been developed for EEG and MEG analysis, with encour-aging results in image recognition, category classification, and visual reconstruction [4], [10], [26], [27], [28], [29]. The setting becomes more demanding when the task shifts from closed set prediction to open vocabulary or zero-shot image retrieval. In retrieval, neural signals must support fine-grained matching within a large candidate set rather than only coarse category discrimination. This change places greater emphasis on semantic structure, representation transferability, and robustness to noise. Compared with direct label prediction, retrieval-based formulations benefit from external semantic spaces that can organize candidate images at the representation level. Existing studies have therefore moved from conventional neural classification toward retrieval-oriented formulations that emphasize semantic correspondence across modalities. These developments indicate that modern neural decoding research increasingly depends on representation spaces that support both semantic generalization and robust matching. 

B. Brain-Visual Shared Semantic Spaces and Cross-Modal Alignment 

Motivated by the success of CLIP-based vision-language representation learning, recent work has explored shared brain-visual semantic spaces in which EEG or MEG responses are projected into pretrained visual embedding spaces [4], [10], [11], [12]. In this line of research, the visual encoder serves as a semantic reference learned from large-scale image-text data, while the neural encoder is trained to map brain responses into the same space. Relative to classification models trained only with neural supervision, this strategy provides richer semantic structure and naturally supports zero-shot retrieval as well as cross-category generalization. Most existing methods optimize this shared space with contrastive objectives that pull matched neural image pairs together and separate mismatched pairs. Recent studies have also explored alternative brain-visual alignment strategies be-yond standard pairwise matching, such as relational cross-modal alignment for visual classification [30]. This design has substantially improved neural-to-image retrieval for both EEG and MEG data and has established cross-modal alignment as a dominant paradigm in the field [4], [10], [13], [12]. At the same time, many current formulations still rely on a relatively uniform projection rule and a shared similarity shaping strategy across heterogeneous neural samples. Since EEG and MEG data are noisy, nonstationary, and subject-dependent, the quality of 3

alignment may vary considerably across trials and subjects, even when the visual space itself is strong. This suggests that preserving alignment quality under heterogeneous neural conditions remains a central challenge in shared brain-visual spaces [31]. 

C. Parameter-Efficient Adaptation and Stable Alignment 

In brain-visual cross-modal learning, the visual branch is usually initialized from a large pretrained model, whereas the neural branch is learned from comparatively small and variable EEG or MEG datasets. Under this data regime, directly fine-tuning the full visual backbone can be computationally expensive and may also weaken the generic semantic struc-ture acquired during large-scale pretraining. For this reason, parameter-efficient transfer strategies have become increasingly relevant [32]. Representative approaches include adapters, low-dimensional adaptation [33], and other lightweight trainable modules that preserve the frozen backbone while adding a limited number of task-specific parameters [34]. Alongside efficient transfer, recent studies have also paid increasing attention to the stability of shared embedding spaces. Standardization, normalization, low-dimensional compression, and correlation-based constraints have been used to reduce feature redundancy, alleviate distributional mismatch, and im-prove the organization of learned representations. These ideas are particularly relevant to EEG and MEG, whose statistical fluctuations can noticeably perturb the geometry of neural embeddings during training. Existing work has shown that lightweight adaptation and embedding regularization can each be beneficial. Related self-supervised representation learning methods, including BYOL [35] and SimSiam [36], also highlight the importance of stabilization and redundancy reduction in learning transferable representations. But they are often intro-duced as separate components with distinct motivations. What remains unclear is how these stabilization and adaptation strate-gies should be organized within a unified retrieval framework. 

D. Frequency-Domain Modeling and Adaptive Supervision 

The frequency-domain structure of EEG and MEG signals contains physiologically meaningful information that is not always explicit in raw time-domain waveforms. Amplitude statistics describe the distribution of spectral energy and rhythmic strength across frequencies, whereas phase statistics reflect temporal synchronization, concentration, and response consistency, and are also closely related to functional connec-tivity analyses in EEG studies [37]. For this reason, frequency-based representations have long been studied as informative descriptors for neural signal analysis [38], [39], [40]. In particular, circular phase statistics provide a compact way to characterize phase concentration and may offer useful cues about the stability of neural responses under noisy conditions. A related line of research concerns adaptive supervision and optimization, where different samples are allowed to contribute unequally during training [41]. Sample weighting, adaptive losses, and hardness-aware strategies have been explored in many learning settings, and these ideas are also relevant to cross-modal neural alignment because response quality can vary across trials and subjects [42], [43]. In the current neural-to-image retrieval literature, frequency-based modeling and adaptive supervision have mostly been studied as separate themes. One emphasizes richer neural descriptors, whereas the other focuses on how training signals should be modulated across samples. To our knowledge, their interaction has been less systematically studied in retrieval-oriented neural alignment. III. Experiments and Results Section IV presents the experimental protocols and the main retrieval results. We first describe the EEG and MEG datasets together with the implementation settings, including optimization details and the visual and neural backbones. We then report the quantitative retrieval performance, followed by qualitative visualization and diagnostic analyses to further interpret the behavior of the proposed framework. 

A. Dataset a) EEG dataset: THINGS-EEG [55] contains recordings from 10 subjects. The training split covers 1,654 object concepts, with 10 images for each concept, and each image is repeated 4 times per subject. The test split contains 200 concepts, with 1 image for each concept, and each test image is repeated 80 times per subject. For preprocessing, we follow the pipeline in [55]. To improve the signal-to-noise ratio, repeated trials elicited by the same stimulus are averaged, resulting in 16,540 training samples and 200 test samples for each subject. 

b) MEG dataset: THINGS-MEG [1] includes recordings from 4 participants acquired with 271 MEG channels. The training split consists of 1,854 concepts, 12 images for each concept, and 1 repetition for each image, whereas the test split consists of 200 concepts, 1 image for each concept, and 12 repetitions for each image. We adopt the same experimental setting and preprocessing protocol as [1]. 

B. Experimented Setup a) Environment And Implementation Details: All exper-iments were implemented in Python 3.8.19 with CUDA 12.0 and PyTorch 2.4.1. Additional dependencies are provided in 

requirements.txt . Training was conducted on a work-station equipped with six NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs, each with 14.5GB of memory. We trained all models for 50 epochs with a batch size of 𝐵 = 1024. Optimization was performed using AdamW [56] with a weight decay of 10 −4. Following the standard protocol, the learning rate was set to 10 −4 for the intra-subject setting and to 10 −5 for the inter-subject setting. Early stopping was performed based on validation performance to mitigate overfitting. 

b) Vision Encoders: For the visual branch, we use pre-trained CLIP image encoders from OpenCLIP [57], [58], [59]. These encoders are trained on large-scale image-text data collected from diverse sources under different training configu-rations, and they provide general-purpose visual representations. In our experiments, we evaluate multiple visual backbones, including RN50, RN101, ViT-B-16, ViT-B-32, ViT-L-14, ViT-H-14, and ViT-g-14. Unless otherwise specified, RN50 is used as the default visual encoder. 

c) Brain Encoders: In the main model, neural signals are encoded by the brain encoder described in the Method section. This encoder serves as the default neural backbone in our framework and is designed for efficient cross-modal alignment in the shared semantic space. To further evaluate the robustness and generalizability of the proposed method, we also consider several alternative EEG backbones, including ShallowNet [26], DeepNet [26], EEGProject [12], EEGNet [27], and TSConv [29]. For all these variants, we append the same projection head to the backbone output so that the comparison focuses on the encoder itself rather than on differences in downstream alignment design.

E. Analysis of Temperature Control Strategies 

To evaluate the proposed phase-guided adaptive temperature mechanism, we compare it with three representative alternatives: a fixed temperature, a global learnable temperature, and simple sample weighting. As reported in Table VII, the fixed tempera-ture provides a strong baseline, whereas the global learnable temperature performs slightly worse. Simple sample weighting yields a modest Top-1 improvement over the fixed-temperature baseline, sug-gesting that the phase-derived cue may provide optimization-relevant information. In contrast, the proposed phase-guided adaptive temperature achieves the best Top-1 accuracy, while the fixed-temperature baseline achieves the highest Top-5 accuracy. These results suggest that adaptive temperature control mainly sharpens the highest-ranked match in the current setting, rather than uniformly improving all ranking depths. VI. Conclusion This work presents a unified framework for neural-to-image retrieval that improves cross-modal matching by stabilizing the neural alignment pathway into a frozen visual semantic space. 11 

Across THINGS-EEG and THINGS-MEG, the proposed design improves retrieval performance under both intra-subject and inter-subject protocols, highlighting the value of jointly integrat-ing representation stabilization, frequency-guided calibration, and adaptive supervision for robust neural-to-image retrieval. 

Limitations : First, the current frequency branch relies on relatively compact statistical descriptors and therefore does not fully capture richer time-frequency structure or finer-grained neural dynamics. Second, although the proposed framework is motivated and analyzed from the perspectives of represen-tational stability and alignment robustness, the reason why the frequency-domain enhancement is especially beneficial for more challenging samples still requires deeper neuroscientific interpretation and stronger statistical validation. Third, the phase-derived control signal should be interpreted primarily as an optimization-control cue rather than as a direct estimator of sample-wise retrieval difficulty, and its relationship to underlying neural reliability remains to be clarified further.
