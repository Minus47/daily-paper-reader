## Abstract 

Neural decoding of visual experience from electroen-cephalography (EEG) has achieved strong performance in intra-subject settings, but current methods fail to generalize across individuals: on the standard 200-way benchmark THINGS-EEG-2, top-1 accuracy drops from above 80% within subjects to about 32% for an unseen subject. Adapting a pre-trained encoder to new subjects and sessions typically requires labeled data, a bottleneck whose collection time severely limits practical device deployment. In a practi-cal Brain-Computer Interface (BCI), however, the possible outcomes are known in advance and form a gallery of can-didates, and the unlabeled EEG of the user accumulates as soon as the device is used. We propose a new setting, 

incremental transductive calibration , in which the decoder is calibrated regularly on all the unlabeled EEG received so far and on the known gallery, without any labels. This setting is closer to the conditions of a deployed BCI than the zero-shot and batch evaluations in current use. We evaluate it with a protocol in which queries arrive one at a time, with unknown and possibly unequal frequencies, and each query is decoded before it is used for calibration. For this setting, we introduce GALA (GA llery-Level Alignment), a label-free calibration method which registers the unlabeled EEG embeddings onto the gallery through shrinkage whitening, hubness correction and a Sinkhorn–Procrustes alignment with query frequencies estimated from the buffer, updated after every query. On THINGS-EEG-2, with a gallery of 200 images, it raises the top-1 accuracy on a new subject from 28% to 67% over 53 minutes of use. Across 60 evaluation settings that vary the gallery size, the query frequencies and the number of repetitions averaged per query, GALA is the most accurate label-free method in every setting, 6.6 points above the best competing method on average over the last 30% of the stream. ## 1. Introduction 

Decoding visual perception from brain activity is a central goal of neuroscience [6, 27, 40], with applications rang-ing from the study of visual representations [11, 13] to as-sistive brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) [23, 46]. EEG is especially attractive for this problem because it is non-invasive, has high temporal resolution, and is relatively in-1arXiv:2611.24206v1 [cs.AI] 18 Nov 2026 expensive. In the now-standard EEG-to-image retrieval set-ting [24, 28, 73], EEG signals and images are represented in a joint embedding space, and retrieval selects, for a given EEG signal, the closest image from a candidate set. Follow-ing progress in fMRI-to-image decoding [59, 67, 68, 74], recent work aligns EEG embeddings with features from large pre-trained vision models through contrastive learn-ing [21, 48, 72, 79, 83], and now exceeds 80% top-1 ac-curacy in the intra-subject 200-way setting [21]. In the 

cross-subject setting, where the encoder must generalize to a held-out subject under the leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) protocol, accuracy remains between 20% and 30% for the strongest published encoders [15, 21, 83]. This gap is rooted in the inter-subject variability typical of EEG: skull geometry, cortical folding and electrode place-ment induce a different spatial mixing of the underlying neural sources for each individual [57], and inter-subject differences in covariance structure further amplify this mis-match [3, 5, 29, 65]. Beyond these anatomical differences, physiological factors (e.g., individual differences in brain activity and cognitive state) and experimental factors (e.g., recording conditions and equipment) further contribute to inter-subject variability. How these differences affect gener-alization to new subjects remains an open question [49, 53]. In a practical use of a brain-computer interface, the set of possible outcomes is known in advance. For example, a person who cannot communicate verbally may use a BCI to select items displayed on a screen, such as food, drinks, or ob-jects they wish to request. The assistive interface presents a predefined set of options, and decoding consists of matching the user’s EEG signal to one of these candidates in the gallery, corresponding to the retrieval setting described above. The main obstacle to deployment is the shift of the EEG signal between subjects, and between sessions of the same sub-ject. Previous methods correct it by fine-tuning on labeled data [43], but acquiring labeled calibration data for each new utilization of the interface is often impractical and time-consuming. A deployed decoder, however, knows its gallery of possible outcomes, and receives unlabeled signal from its user as soon as it is used. Current evaluations do not reflect this situation. The THINGS-EEG-2 test set was designed for zero-shot evalu-ation: it contains 200 images, each paired with one EEG query averaged over 80 repetitions, and every query is de-coded alone. Most recent label-free test-time adaptation methods [15, 34] are evaluated in a very similar setting, in which the 200 queries are available at once and every gallery image is queried exactly once. We therefore propose a new evaluation setting, incremental transductive calibra-tion , which simulates the deployment of a BCI: as the user operates the interface, its unlabeled EEG accumulates, and at every step the decoder is calibrated on all the EEG re-ceived so far, together with the known gallery (Fig. 1). Our contributions are summarized as follows: 1. We introduce incremental transductive calibration, a re-alistic setting in which the decoder is calibrated at every step on the growing buffer of unlabeled EEG and the known gallery, and an evaluation protocol for it, in which every query is decoded before it is used for calibration, with skewed query frequencies and partially queried gal-leries. 2. We introduce GALA, which casts calibration as a point-cloud registration problem [4, 26] regularized by the zero-shot decoder as a prior, estimates the query frequencies from the buffer and is updated after every query. Across 60 evaluation settings on THINGS-EEG-2, it is the most accurate label-free method in every setting over the last 30% of the stream, 6.6 points above the best competing method on average. 3. We also evaluate GALA in the standard batch setting, in which it reaches 79.1% top-1 cross-subject accuracy without any labeled data from the new subject, 15.8 points above a Hungarian assignment that uses only the one-to-one structure of this test. The full training and evaluation code, as well as the con-figuration files for every reported run and the trained check-points, will be released publicly. 

## 2. Related Work 

EEG-to-image decoding. Visual neural decoding has pro-gressed from early supervised, subject-specific classifiers on labeled EEG-image pairs [20, 73] to a zero-shot retrieval paradigm enabled by large-scale serial visual presentation benchmarks [24, 28]. NICE [72] established the contrastive alignment framework, using an InfoNCE [75] objective to align TSConv EEG embeddings with frozen CLIP [62] im-age features. UBP [79] dynamically blurs image targets pro-portional to stimulus uncertainty, while NeuroBridge [83] introduced principled image augmentation for better bidi-rectional alignment. ENIGMA [43] adapts to new subjects in a few-shot setting through subject-specific latent layers coupled with a compact bottleneck. Shallow Alignment [21] showed that intermediate-layer features of large pretrained vi-sion models [9] better match the granularity of EEG, achiev-ing 82.6% intra-subject and 21.8% cross-subject top-1 accu-racy. 

Cross-subject generalization. Inter-subject variability has long been identified as the central bottleneck for generaliz-able BCI systems [37, 55, 65]. Training-time approaches such as adversarial inference [60] and multi-source distri-bution matching [7, 69] improve robustness for paradigm-specific encoders, but do not provide a label-free correction at test time. EEG foundation models [14, 22] aim at transfer-able representations, but do not explicitly address the align-2ment between neural signals and a visual embedding space. Riemannian re-centering [82], Euclidean alignment [31] and Riemannian Procrustes analysis [64] align the covariance structure of a new subject without labels. These methods align the target to itself or to source statistics, ignoring the fact that in a retrieval setting, the gallery provides a second, fixed point cloud in the embedding space. 

Label-free calibration for EEG-to-image retrieval. 

More recent work proposes label-free calibration methods for retrieval, such as SATTC [34], which combines subject-adaptive whitening with adaptive local scaling and a struc-tural expert, and reports an improvement from 9.2% to 14.8% top-1. SCORE [15] observes that subjects preserve concept relationships along different coordinate directions, trains the encoder on simulated recovery episodes, and at deploy-ment estimates one orthogonal map from hubness-corrected mutual nearest neighbours, reaching 53.2%. Both are trans-ductive in the target data, and are evaluated on the 200-image test set, with all queries available at once and one query per image. 

Transductive inference and test-time adaptation. Test-time adaptation updates normalization statistics or parame-ters on unlabeled target batches [36, 50, 52, 77], and trans-ductive vision-language methods exploit the joint structure of a query batch [56, 81]. In the past, evaluations of these meth-ods have repeatedly shown that gains measured on curated test batches do not survive realistic class imbalance, small batches or model selection without target labels [76, 84]. We apply these lessons to brain decoding. 

Unsupervised adaptation during use. Adapting a de-coder on the unlabeled data of its user has a long history in BCI: event-related potential spellers can be trained during use, without a calibration session, from the constraints of the application [42] or from known label proportions [35]. In machine learning, online test-time adaptation updates a model on a stream of unlabeled inputs [25, 78], and label-shift methods estimate unknown class frequencies from the predictions of a fixed classifier [54]. The setting we study differs from these lines of work in four respects: the task is retrieval over a gallery of embeddings that is known in advance, the calibration is estimated from a growing buffer, each query is decoded before it enters this buffer, and the query frequencies are unknown and may leave a part of the gallery unqueried. 

Hubness and transductive alignment. Hubness [63], the tendency of a small subset of target vectors to dom-inate nearest-neighbor lists in high-dimensional spaces, is exacerbated when mapping source vectors between different domains [19]. Cross-domain Similarity Local Scaling (CSLS) [12] addresses this issue by penalizing frequently-retrieved points, and is one of the components of SATTC [34]. In unsupervised word translation, Conneau et al. [12] combine adversarial initialization with orthogonal Procrustes refinement [66], Artetxe et al. [2] iterate CSLS re-trieval and orthogonal mapping in a self-learning loop, Grave et al. [26] cast the problem as Wasserstein Procrustes [61] using Sinkhorn correspondence estimation [16], and Alvarez-Melis and Jaakkola [1] use Gromov-Wasserstein distances. Functional alignment of brain responses across subjects follows the same principle with labeled, time-locked stim-uli [8, 30, 68]. We follow the same line of work, but trans-pose it to EEG-to-image retrieval, where the anchor of the alignment is the fixed image set, and the cloud of source data that we want to align grows during use. 

## 3. Incremental Transductive Calibration 

Problem formulation. We consider a multi-subject EEG-to-image decoding setting. Let xs,i ∈ RC×T denote the EEG signal recorded from subject s ∈ S in response to image i ∈ I , where C is the number of EEG channels and 

T the number of time steps, and let vi ∈ Rd denote the corresponding image embedding. We observe a dataset D =

{(xs,i , vi) : s ∈ S , i ∈ I} where multiple subjects provide distinct EEG responses to the same visual stimulus. An encoder Eθ : RC×T → Rd is trained on the source subjects 

S and then frozen. At test time, a new subject s⋆ /∈ S 

views images from a known gallery V = {v1, . . . , vK },and the decoder receives unlabeled queries et = Eθ (xs⋆,i t ),

t = 1 , 2, . . . , whose stimulus indices it are unknown. At step t, the decoder holds the buffer Qt = {e1, . . . , et} of all the queries received so far. An incremental transductive calibration is a sequence of corrections ct = C(Qt; V),each computed from the whole buffer and the gallery only. The next query et+1 is decoded with ct, before it enters the buffer. The correction must be defined for every buffer size: for an empty buffer it is the identity, which gives the zero-shot decoder, and at the start of a session it is estimated from few queries in a d-dimensional space. Calibration is specific to a subject and session. It requires no label and no dedicated calibration phase, since it uses the EEG that the user produces while naturally operating the interface. 

Assumptions. The setting relies on four assumptions: • (A1) Known gallery: the set of candidates is known in ad-vance. Zero-shot retrieval already makes this assumption, since the gallery is needed to rank the candidates. • (A2) Growing unlabeled buffer: the queries of the user are stored, and the calibration at step t uses all of them. • (A3) Unknown query frequencies: the user does not query every gallery image equally often, and the frequen-cies are unknown to the decoder. 3• (A4) Partial coverage: at any step, and possibly over the whole session, some gallery images have never been queried. 

Standard evaluations. Prior work on THINGS-EEG-2 uses two evaluations (Fig. 1). In the zero-shot evaluation, each of the 200 test queries is decoded alone. In the batch 

evaluation, the 200 queries are calibrated together, and each gallery image is queried exactly once. The batch evaluation is the limiting case of our setting in which the buffer is complete, with a bijection between queries and images in place of (A3) and (A4). This bijection alone is informative: a one-to-one (Hungarian) assignment [45] of the zero-shot scores already raises accuracy substantially (Sec. 5.7). 

Evaluation protocol. We simulate a session of use from the repetitions of the THINGS-EEG-2 test set. A query is the average of q repetitions of one gallery image, such that the 80 repetitions of an image provide 80 /q disjoint queries. We consider three streams, which differ in how often each gallery image is queried. In the uniform stream, every image is queried 80 /q times. In the skewed stream, the number of queries per image decreases exponentially from 80 /q to 1. In the partial stream, a random quarter of the gallery is never queried, and the number of queries per image decreases exponentially from 80 /q to 1 over the other images. Queries arrive one at a time in random order, which mixes the four recording sessions. Every query is decoded once, by the calibration fitted on the queries received before it, and the calibration is updated after every query [18]. The first five queries are decoded by the zero-shot decoder. The metric is the top-1 accuracy of these decisions, reported against the stimulus time elapsed. Methods that only decode the batch they are fitted on (SATTC, TransCLIP, EM-Dirichlet and the Hungarian assignment) are fitted on the buffer extended with the incoming query, and we keep their decision for this query. In this setting, the gallery V is a fixed point cloud in the embedding space, and the buffer of the new subject forms a second, growing cloud. If the encoder places each stimulus at a consistent position for every subject, up to a subject-specific transformation, the query cloud is a distorted copy of the queried part of the gallery. Calibration then amounts to registering the query cloud onto the gallery without known correspondences. Sec. 4 describes how GALA estimates this registration.

## 5. Experiments 

5.1. Dataset and Experimental Setup 

THINGS-EEG-2 [24] contains EEG recordings from 10 healthy participants who viewed images from 1,654 training-set object concepts and 200 testing-set concepts drawn from the THINGS database [32]. Images are shown for 100 ms followed by a 100 ms blank screen. Each test concept is rep-resented by one test image, recorded 80 times over four ses-sions. Our data preprocessing pipeline follows the method-ology described in previous work [48, 72, 79]. 

Evaluation. The evaluation task is 200-way closed-set re-trieval : given the averaged EEG response of a held-out subject to one of the 200 test images, rank all 200 candi-date images and report top-1 and top-5 accuracy (chance = 0.5% and 2.5% respectively). In the incremental setting, the gallery is the same 200 images, a query averages q = 20 

repetitions unless stated otherwise, and we follow the pro-tocol of Sec. 3 with ten random stream orders per subject. With q = 20 , the uniform, skewed and partial streams last 53, 29 and 21 minutes of stimulus time. The accuracy after 

m minutes is computed on the queries received between 

0.7m and 1.3m minutes, and the accuracy at the end of a stream on its last 10% of queries. We adopt the leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) protocol: train on 9 subjects, evaluate on the 10th, and repeat for all 10 folds. Much of this line of work [21, 34, 83] selects the best checkpoint on the test-set loss of the held-out subject, which uses the test subject for model selection. Baselines. Among the label-free calibrations baselines, SATTC is evaluated with its official released code. SCORE has no released code at the time of submission, so we repro-duce it from the paper, with its own encoder (SAMGA [38] with recovery-aware training) and its deployment step. This reproduction reaches 52.4% in the batch evaluation, against 53.2% reported (Sec. 5.7). Our experiments with SCORE have revealed that the method is considerably stronger when coupled with ATM [48] than with the SAMGA encoder originally used by the authors. For fairness, we also report SCORE in this stronger setting. As additional baselines, we add CSLS alone, the transductive vision-language methods EM-Dirichlet [56] and TransCLIP [81], with their official solvers. From test-time adaptation, we add AdaBN [50] and Tent [77], which update the normalization layers of the en-coder on the raw EEG of the buffer, every 20 queries. Finally, we add the Hungarian assignment [45] as a control, in which every gallery image receives the same number of queries: it uses the one-to-one structure of the batch test and no other information. 

5.2. Base encoder training 

All calibrations act on frozen encoders trained with the same recipe: a multi-positive contrastive objective on stimulus-grouped batches [41], in which each trial is mixed with the trial of another source subject for the same stimulus. The objective is detailed in Appendix B. 

5.3. Implementation Details 

Our method is implemented using PyTorch, and each model is trained on one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 GPU. We employ a batch size of 1,024, AdamW with a learning rate of 3 × 10 −4, and train the models for 50 epochs (100 for ATM), with early stopping after 20 epochs without improve-ment of the validation loss. The temperature parameter 

τ is set to 0.07. Our main encoder is ATM [48] with a 128-dimensional embedding, trained with three seeds. All encoders are aligned to intermediate-layer (layer 28) fea-tures of InternViT-6B [9], following Shallow Alignment [21]. For Sec. 5.6, we train four more encoders with one seed: TSConv [72], EEGNet [46], EEGProject [21] and EEG Con-former [71]. Finally, we fully fine-tune LaBraM [39], a pretrained EEG foundation model, with a contrastive and regression objective, following the protocol of AVDE [17]. We also train ATM with one seed against five other image targets to study GALA’s robustness to different alignment objectives (Appendix C). We systematically use a CSLS neighborhood k = 3 , 12 Sinkhorn normalization steps, a pseudo-count nb = 1 and one iteration per update, and select the shrinkage coefficient ρ, the Sinkhorn temperature τsk and the power γ, as well as the two hyper-parameters of SCORE with ATM, cross-fold over a grid on the streams of the other subjects. The prior is set to n0 = 2 d observations for every subject and every stream. The calibration runs on a standard CPU: on one core, one update of GALA takes 15 ms with a buffer of 400 queries and 24 ms with 800 queries, about ten times less than 16 iterations from the identity, which reach the same accuracy within 0.3 points (Table 4). 

5.4. Main Results 

Figure 3 and Table 1 compare GALA to existing approaches in the incremental setting. GALA is the most accurate method at every reported time of the three streams. After one minute, when the buffer holds about 15 queries, GALA and CSLS are the two most accurate methods. After five minutes, SCORE with ATM is the second-best method, 3.9 points below GALA on the uniform stream. It then grows more slowly, while GALA keeps improving with the size 7of the buffer: GALA reaches 59.1% on the uniform stream after 20 minutes, and 67.3% at the end of the stream, 13.9 points above SCORE. SATTC, TransCLIP and EM-Dirichlet stay at or below 40% after 20 minutes. Appendix A reports the same comparison for other menu sizes and numbers of repetitions: across a total of 60 evaluation settings with dif-ferent gallery sizes, numbers of repetitions and stream types, GALA is the most accurate method over the last 30% of the stream in every setting, by 6.6 points on average (Fig. 4). 

5.5. Ablation Study 

Table 4 (Appendix G) shows the individual contribution of each GALA component. The rotation is the main component: without it, accuracy after 20 minutes drops from 59.1% to 39.1% on the uniform stream. Without the prior, GALA is far below the zero-shot decoder after one minute, at 10.7% against 30.1% on the uniform stream. Whitening and CSLS each bring 1.6 to 3.7 points after 20 minutes. For the column masses of the plan, we compare the estimated frequencies of GALA with uniform masses and with a plan without column constraint. After 20 minutes, the estimated frequencies are 0.5 to 1.1 points above both alternatives on the three streams. 

5.6. Generalization to Other Encoders and Datasets 

To assess the robustness of our framework, we run the in-cremental protocol on five other EEG encoder architectures (Appendix F). GALA is above SCORE for every encoder at every reported time, except for LaBraM after 10 and 20 minutes. As the size of the gain depends strongly on the encoder, we provide an analysis in Appendix C that relates it to the similarity between the representational geometries of the EEG and image embeddings. Finally, we also run the incremental protocol with ATM on Alljoined-1.6M [80] and THINGS-MEG [33] (Appendix E). On these datasets, the zero-shot accuracy is much weaker due to a low signal-to-noise ratio, and every label-free method, including GALA, stays within 4 points of it. 

5.7. Standard Evaluations 

Table 2 (Appendix D) reports the standard zero-shot and batch evaluations on the 200-image test set, with one 80-repetition query per image. In this evaluation, the buffer is complete, and GALA runs without the prior, with 16 iterations from the identity. It raises top-1 accuracy from 32.2% to 79.1%, and is better than every other label-free method we evaluate for every subject.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusion 

Evaluation practice. Cross-subject EEG-to-image decod-ing is currently evaluated in conditions that are far from deployment, either without adaptation or with the whole test set at once, and accuracy on unseen subjects remains low. Incremental transductive calibration evaluates decoders in conditions closer to deployment, and GALA raises cross-subject accuracy over existing label-free methods in all the evaluated settings. Our experiments also point to two further limitations of current practice. First, THINGS-EEG-2 pro-vides no validation set, and much of the literature [21, 34, 83] selects the checkpoint on the held-out subject, which inflates the zero-shot accuracy of ATM by 3.6 points. We select all checkpoints on held-out training concepts. Second, a Hun-garian assignment that uses only the one-to-one structure of the standard test set raises accuracy from 32.2% to 63.3%, and results on this test set should be reported together with this control. 

Limitations and future work. GALA’s advantage de-pends on the base decoder: with LaBraM, SCORE is up to 1.7 points more accurate than GALA between 5 and 20 min-utes of use (Appendix F), and the gain is small on Alljoined-1.6M and THINGS-MEG, where the zero-shot accuracy is below 10%. Our evaluation, following the benchmark it builds on, replays offline recordings of a rapid serial visual presentation paradigm, in which each query averages many repetitions of the same image. A deployed BCI rarely offers these conditions: the candidate set changes with the task of the user, and queries come from few repetitions. Evalua-tions that reproduce these conditions, with task-dependent candidate sets, closed-loop sessions and single-trial queries, would measure more realistically what transductive calibra-tion brings in practice. 

Conclusion. We argue for gallery-level alignment as a way to deploy EEG decoders on new users without labeled cali-bration data, formalize incremental transductive calibration, the assumptions under which it should be evaluated, and introduce GALA. Across 60 evaluation settings, GALA is the most accurate label-free method over the last 30% of the stream in every setting. We hope that these contributions will help put forward incremental transductive calibration as a more realistic and practical alternative to the current bench-marking methods and advance research towards real-world BCI deployment. 8LLM Usage Statement

## C. Calibration Gain across Encoders and Image Targets 

Encoders and image targets. In the batch evaluation (Sec. 5.7), GALA fixes 36% to 71% of the zero-shot er-rors of the six encoders. Despite a correlation between base zero-shot accuracy and the gain yielded by GALA, it is not always the case (Fig. 5a): for instance, ATM and TSConv have roughly the same zero-shot accuracy, but GALA fixes 71% of the errors of ATM and only 45% of those of TSConv. As an additional diagnostic, we measure the representational similarity (RSA) [44] between the two modalities, as the correlation between the pairwise cosine similarities of the centered test EEG embeddings and those of their images. Two clouds with identical similarity matrices are related by an orthogonal map, so RSA measures how close the EEG cloud is to a rotated copy of the image cloud, which is pre-cisely the geometric mismatch that GALA aims to recover. Over the six encoders, ATM has the highest RSA and the largest gain. To widen the range of our experiment, we train ATM against five other image targets: intermediate layers of InternViT-6B (layer 20) and OpenCLIP bigG [10] (layer 27), and the final or near-final layers of CLIP ViT-H [62], InternViT-6B (layer 44) and DINOv2 [58] (layer 40). Over the 11 models, RSA predicts the gain better than zero-shot accuracy (Spearman correlation of 0.95 against 0.83). 

## D. Standard Batch Evaluation 

Comparison with published results. The published cal-ibration results mix the calibration with the base encoder. SCORE reports 53.2% with its own encoder, SAMGA [38]. Its code is not released, so we reproduced its training on the official SAMGA code and reimplemented its deploy-ment step. Run with its official code under its own protocol, SATTC only reaches 11.4%. We instead replicated their calibration protocol with the strongest encoder available for a fairer evaluation.
