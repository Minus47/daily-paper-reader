# EEG-Based Prediction of Music Enjoyment Using Deep Learning with Explainable Temporal Analysis 

## Parshav Singla 1, Simranjit Kaur 1* , Maanya Walia 1, Niharika 1

> 1*

Abstract 

Understanding why listeners enjoy music is difficult because the experience is subjective and changes as a piece unfolds. Electroencephalography (EEG) offers a non-invasive way to study neural responses during listening, yet earlier work has mostly treated music emotion as a categorical label or relied on black-box models. We formulate enjoyment prediction as a regression problem on the public MUSIN-G dataset, which contains 128-channel EEG from 20 listeners together with song-level enjoyment ratings. Six deep learning models were compared under a participant-wise protocol, in which 15 listeners were used for training and validation and 5 were held out for testing. DeepConvNet, CNN-LSTM and GNN-CNN reached the lowest mean absolute error (0.97 to 0.99 on the 1 to 5 scale), while the temporal convolutional network (TCN) had the highest Pearson correlation with the ratings (r = 0.21). Gradient-based and occlusion-based explanations, averaged over all held-out windows, identify the time segments within each two-second window that most influence the TCN predictions. The study provides a reproducible cross-listener benchmark for EEG-based enjoyment regression and shows how explainability analysis complements standard metrics.  

> Keywords: Electroencephalography, Music enjoyment, Deep learning, Temporal modeling, Explainable AI, Regression

# 1 Introduction 

Music enjoyment is a deeply personal and tem-porally evolving experience, heavily influenced by rhythm, melody, familiarity, and individual pref-erence. Understanding how these acoustic features translate into cortical activity remains an open challenge for affective computing and cognitive neuroscience. Electroencephalography (EEG) pro-vides a valuable, non-invasive window into these neural dynamics as listeners process music. How-ever, decoding subjective enjoyment directly from EEG data is notoriously difficult. Factors such as inherent signal noise, substantial differences in brain activity across individuals, and the lack of explicit ground-truth labels at a fine temporal scale make this a highly complex task (Miyapuram et al. 2022). Recently, advances in deep learning have dra-matically improved our ability to model EEG data by learning spatial and temporal patterns end-to-end. Convolutional architectures excel at mapping spatial dependencies across the scalp, 1arXiv:2610.20795v1 [cs.LG] 14 Oct 2026 while temporal models are highly effective at cap-turing sequential neural dynamics as they unfold over time (Lawhern et al. 2018; Schirrmeister et al. 2017). Despite these advances, the vast majority of music-related EEG research remains limited to predicting broad categorical emotions or classifying rhythms. These studies often rely on randomized trial-level data splits that artifi-cially inflate how well the model generalizes to new listeners (Wang et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2024). Fur-thermore, complex deep learning approaches are frequently treated as black boxes, offering little insight into exactly how specific neural patterns relate to the listener’s actual experience of music. In this work, we address these limitations by formulating EEG-based music enjoyment as a continuous regression problem, rigorously eval-uated under a stringent participant-wise proto-col. Using the MUSIN-G dataset, which contains high-density EEG recordings from 20 participants with corresponding self-reported enjoyment rat-ings (Miyapuram et al. 2022), we systematically benchmark convolutional, temporal, and hybrid neural architectures. Crucially, to move beyond simple predictive metrics, we integrate explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques to analyze the temporal relevance of EEG segments. Our goal is not just to build an accurate predictive model, but to uncover how sustained neural dynamics correlate with the subjective human experience of music enjoyment. The main contributions of this paper are as fol-lows: (1) Music enjoyment is formulated as a con-tinuous regression task under a strict participant-wise protocol emphasizing cross-subject gener-alization. (2) Six architectures spanning convo-lutional, temporal, recurrent, and graph-based designs are systematically compared under iden-tical conditions. (3) Explainability is treated as a core analytical goal through gradient-based, saliency-based, and occlusion-based XAI methods. 

# 2 Related Work 

## 2.1 EEG-Based Affective Computing 

The study of EEG-based music perception has a rich history in affective computing. Early neuro-physiological research focused heavily on spectral analysis of canonical EEG bands during music listening, discovering clear links between neural oscillations and emotional engagement mapped to arousal and valence spaces (Russell 1980; Koel-stra et al. 2012). Traditional machine learning pipelines later utilized handcrafted features for music emotion recognition, but these methods fre-quently struggled to generalize across different subjects (Alarcao and Fonseca 2019). Deep learning approaches have since trans-formed the field (Zheng and Lu 2015). Convo-lutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have enabled robust hierarchical feature learning directly from multichannel EEG signals (Schirrmeister et al. 2017; Lawhern et al. 2018), while hybrid archi-tectures were developed to explicitly capture temporal dependencies. More recently, Tempo-ral Convolutional Networks (TCNs) have shown exceptional performance in capturing long-range temporal structures using dilated convolutions with stable optimization (Ingolfsson et al. 2020). Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have also been explored for modeling complex functional con-nectivity between EEG channels, although their robustness in cross-subject evaluations remains challenging (Song et al. 2020). 

## 2.2 Music Enjoyment and Preference Modeling 

While categorical emotion recognition has been extensively researched, the neural signatures underlying subjective music enjoyment and pref-erence are distinct and often obscured by standard affective models. Foundational studies by Lin et al. (2010) and by Hadjidimitriou and Hadjileontiadis (2012) were among the first to show the feasibility of classifying music liking through time-frequency analysis. Furthermore, recent evidence suggests that inter-subject neural synchrony strongly corre-lates with sustained musical engagement (Madsen et al. 2019). Despite these advances, highly subjec-tive constructs like enjoyment are still frequently discretized into simple categories in the EEG literature, stripping away the nuanced temporal variation of the listening experience (Wang et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2024). This study directly bridges this gap by prioritizing continuous, regression-based enjoyment prediction supported by robust explainability. 23 Dataset and Problem Formulation

MUSIN-G Dataset. The Music Listening– Genre EEG dataset (MUSIN-G) was collected at the Indian Institute of Technology Gandhinagar and is publicly available at OpenNeuro (Miya-puram et al. 2022). It provides continuous EEG recordings from 20 participants listening to music across diverse genres, accompanied by self-reported familiarity and enjoyment ratings. 

## 3.1 MUSIN-G Dataset Details 

The MUSIN-G dataset contains recordings from 20 participants who listened to 12 song excerpts of about two minutes each spanning multiple gen-res. EEG data were recorded using a 128-channel system under eyes-closed conditions to minimize ocular artifacts. Following each song, participants rated their overall enjoyment on a standard 1–5 Likert scale (Miyapuram et al. 2022). 

## 3.2 Regression Formulation 

Let X ∈ RC×T denote an EEG segment featur-ing C channels and T temporal samples, and let 

y ∈ [1 , 5] denote the participant’s corresponding enjoyment rating. The objective is to learn a map-ping fθ (X) = ˆ y, where ˆ y accurately approximates the ground-truth enjoyment score. Ratings are normalized to [0 , 1] during the training phase and remapped to the original 1–5 scale during evalua-tion. To prevent subject-level data leakage, a strict participant-wise split (15 participants for training and validation, 5 completely held out for testing) is enforced across all evaluations (Brookshire et al. 2024). 

# 4 Methodology 

## 4.1 EEG Preprocessing 

Raw EEG signals are first average-referenced and high-pass filtered at 0.2 Hz. The data are then cleaned using Independent Component Analysis (ICA), applying MARA-based artifact rejection via EEGLAB. The cleaned data are downsam-pled to 250 Hz, bad channels are interpolated via spherical splines, and continuous recordings are segmented into fixed-length, non-overlapping win-dows of 500 samples. Because enjoyment labels are provided per song, all windows derived from the same song inherit the same label. This weak-label approach is effective for temporal modeling, but introduces some label noise as human enjoy-ment naturally fluctuates across a full song (Wang et al. 2023). ## 4.2 Loss Function and Optimization 

Models are optimized using a combined regression loss that balances sensitivity to large prediction errors with robustness against minor outliers, as formulated in Equation 1: 

L = α · L MSE + (1 − α) · L MAE , (1) where α = 0 .6. The individual components are standard Mean Squared Error and Mean Absolute Error evaluations between ground truth ( y) and prediction (ˆ y). The models are optimized using Adam and AdamW optimizers with an initial learning rate of 3 × 10 −4, utilizing CosineAnnealing scheduling for stable convergence. Dropout rates of 0.5–0.6 and early stopping after 5–7 epochs of no valida-tion improvement are applied. The full training configuration is listed in Table 1.

# 5 Models Evaluated 

To ensure a comprehensive analysis, a diverse set of deep learning architectures was benchmarked under the same preprocessing pipeline and evalu-ation protocol. 

EEGNet (Lawhern et al. 2018) serves as a lightweight, parameter-efficient baseline model known for its stability on smaller EEG datasets. ShallowConvNet and DeepCon-vNet (Schirrmeister et al. 2017) were selected as spatial baselines for their proven ability to extract oscillatory energy patterns and build deep hierarchical features across the temporal and spatial domains. CNN–LSTM merges convo-lutional spatial extraction with recurrent tem-poral memory, enabling modeling of sequential dynamics at the cost of increased training com-plexity. TCN (Ingolfsson et al. 2020) explicitly models long-range temporal dependencies using dilated causal convolutions while maintaining stable optimization. GNN–CNN (Song et al. 2020) explores inter-channel functional connectiv-ity through graph convolutions using a predefined spatial adjacency matrix based on the 128-channel EEG montage. # 6 Experiments and Results 

## 6.1 Evaluation Protocol and Metrics 

All models were evaluated exclusively using the 15-train / 5-test participant-wise split, prevent-ing any subject-level information leakage. Perfor-mance is assessed using the four metrics described in Section 4: MAE, RMSE, Pearson r, and toler-ance accuracy. 

## 6.2 Regression Performance on MUSIN-G 

The results, detailed in Table 4, underscore the inherent difficulty of cross-subject enjoyment pre-diction. Deep convolutional models achieved the lowest absolute error (MAE ≈ 0.97–0.99). On the 1–5 Likert scale, this corresponds roughly to an adjacent ordinal disagreement, which is expected in unpersonalized cross-subject tasks. Temporal models showed a distinct complementary strength: the TCN achieved the highest Pearson correla-tion ( r = 0 .21), demonstrating its superior ability to capture relative enjoyment trends as a song progresses. # 7 Discussion and Limitations 

Our findings confirm that EEG signals contain measurable, contextually rich information regard-ing subjective music enjoyment, aligning with recent advances in affective computing (Wang et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2024). The superior trend alignment of temporal architectures underscores the need to treat music enjoyment as an evolv-ing sequence rather than a static state. The XAI analyses further support this by showing that prediction quality depends on accumulated tem-poral context rather than any single brief neural event, consistent with neurophysiological accounts of music perception (Madsen et al. 2019). The negative Pearson correlation for EEGNet and ShallowConvNet suggests that these compact models lack the representational capacity needed to track relative enjoyment trends, even when their absolute errors are moderate. The GNN– CNN model, despite achieving the best tolerance accuracy, shows a limited positive correlation, suggesting that graph-based representations may be too subject-specific to transfer reliably across participants without personalization. Several limitations must be acknowledged. The relatively small participant pool in MUSIN-G constrains statistical power. Song-level labels applied to two-second windows introduce unavoid-able weak-label noise, as human enjoyment nat-urally fluctuates within a piece. Relying on EEG alone fails to account for contextual factors such as cultural background, musical familiarity, and autobiographical memory. To mitigate the effects of weak-label noise, future iterations will explore multi-instance learning (MIL) paradigms or frame-level attention gating mechanisms to dynamically weight the relevance of individual temporal windows rather than utilizing uniform label assignment. Future work should incorpo-rate larger cohorts, multimodal integration, and personalized fine-tuning to improve absolute pre-dictive performance. 

# 8 Conclusion 

This study presents a rigorous, regression-based and explainability-aware framework for predicting music enjoyment from EEG data. By systemat-ically benchmarking six deep learning architec-tures on the MUSIN-G dataset under a strict participant-wise protocol, we demonstrate that EEG signals support meaningful cross-subject enjoyment prediction, while no single architec-ture simultaneously minimizes absolute error and maximizes trend alignment. Deep convolutional models minimize absolute error, while TCN best captures relative enjoyment trends. Integrated XAI analysis confirms that predictions rely on distributed temporal EEG dynamics, providing a transparent foundation for future work in person-alized, neurologically driven affective computing.
